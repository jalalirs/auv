# The third plan: five places, one platform

What `r2.md` got structurally wrong, and this corrects: it made Looe Key a
*step*. Step 7 rebuilt Looe Key, step 8 built a library from what Looe Key
taught, and the other four places were swept up at the end in step 11. That is
not a plan for five places. It is a plan for one place with four afterthoughts,
and it happened because Looe Key is the only site anybody has ever fetched
evidence for.

So this plan separates two things the last one confused:

  **The machinery** — materials, assets, life, ground, the renderer. Built
  once, and every place gets it.

  **The evidence** — what each place is actually made of. Per place, and
  nobody else's.

Everything still owed from `r2.md` is carried forward here, with its status.
Nothing is dropped and nothing is quietly restarted.

## Where the five places actually stand

The numbers that forced this rewrite:

| | ground | colonies | cover | cover checked? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **looe-key** | NOAA lidar, 2 m, 8 surveys | 83,055 / km² | **4.8%** | **yes** — CREMP, September |
| **thuwal-deep** | GMRT synthesis, 15.7 m | 20,000 | 3.3% | correct: it is 550 m down |
| **al-fahal** | Sentinel-2 derived, 5.9 m | 44,000 / km² | 9.7% | no |
| **kaneohe** | NOAA topobathy lidar, 3.4 m | 500,000 / km² | 22.3% | no |
| **red-sea** | composed | **1,400,000 / km²** | **41.7%** | no — and it is composed |
| **mhl-tank** | a published tank | none | none | it is a tank |

**Looe Key has seventeen times fewer colonies per square kilometre than Red
Sea**, because Looe Key is the only place where somebody went and looked. In
September a reef claiming 86% stony cover met a survey saying 3.4% and was
rebuilt. The other three still carry the number that was thrown out.

The *cover* column is the first time that complaint has been a measurement.
It is what `reef.cover_over` gets over the ground each place's colonies
actually sit on: colonies spread over the five-metre cells they cover,
saturated per cell as `1 - exp(-A/G)` because they are scattered rather than
tiled, averaged over the cells that have reef in them. One function, called by
both reef builders as they build and by `tools/deliver` on the colonies it
reads back out of the published USD.

Looe Key's 4.8% is the one to read the rest against: summed from 83,055
detections in the USGS SQUID-5 DEM, against CREMP's 3.4–4% from transects on
the same reef. Two surveys, two methods, agreeing to within the question of
whether the detector's "low" class is stony coral — a question about a
classifier, not about either number.

Against that, **Kaneohe at 22% and Red Sea at 42% are four and nine times the
one place anybody has counted**, and Red Sea carries 63% of its ground in
colony area before overlap comes out. Al Fahal's 9.7% is twice Looe Key rather
than an order above it. None of the three has a survey to be wrong against;
what has changed is that the size of the claim is in a file anyone can open.

**This column was published wrong twice before it was right, and the shape of
both errors is worth keeping.** The first divided by every five-metre cell
with a colony in it, which is only the reef's ground where colonies fill a
cell — Al Fahal read 9.1% against a true 52.7% at the time, and the error
flattered exactly the three places whose density nobody has checked. Looe Key
agreed with its transects either way, because its survey tiles really are
nearly filled, and one place agreeing was taken as a check when it is not one.
The second added colony areas up without saturating overlap and Red Sea came
back 110.78% covered — not a number, and the only one wrong by enough to be
obviously impossible.

**What remains, and it is not a fault.** `deliver` and the builders still come
out 1.3% apart at Al Fahal and 1.8% at Thuwal Deep. Cover has a threshold in
it — a cell is reef above 2% — and a threshold is a knife edge: at Al Fahal
968 cells of 79,000 sit within a rounding of it and flip between two
computations that agree about every colony. At Red Sea, which is dense, one
does. So the integrity check is on **colony area**, which has no threshold in
it and matches to one part in a million on all five: every published file is
the reef it was built as, and any cover statistic computed from it afterwards
is a question about the statistic.

**And only Looe Key has a reference corpus at all.** One directory, 186 MB:
bathymetry, habitat polygons, CREMP tables, 47 photographs, species counts, and
one Smithsonian coral scan. The other four have a published package and nothing
behind it. The USGS photogrammetry for Looe Key — the 5 mm orthomosaic, the
1 cm DEM, the point cloud — is catalogued with its DOI and **not fetched: 52
files, 97 GB.**

## The rule, carried forward unchanged

**No step is done until it has a screen.** It held for the last plan and it
caught step 18 half-finished. It holds here.

**And nothing invented is allowed in.** Colonies from scans, cover from
surveys, water from measured coefficients, weather from a service that measured
it. Where a number cannot be sourced it is a parameter with its uncertainty
beside it. The colony counts above are what happens when this slips.

# Phase A · The machinery every place gets

Built once. Judged on all five, not on whichever one looks best.

## A1 · Colonies that were scanned

There is one real coral in this repository: `usnm_58_orbicella_coronata.glb`,
a Smithsonian CC0 type specimen, 100,000 triangles with UVs, normals and three
texture maps. It has been sitting in Looe Key's reference since September,
unused, while every colony in every frame is a procedural blob.

  **Use it**, as the mounding prototype. *Orbicella* is the dominant
  reef-builder at Looe Key and a mounding coral is the commonest thing in
  frame everywhere.

  **Fetch the rest.** The Smithsonian's 3D API is CC0 and needs no account;
  the reference tool already proves the path. They are dry skeletons, so they
  give form and not colour — the colour comes from the photographs each place
  has, which is a per-place question and belongs in Phase B.

  **And keep the procedural ones** for what scans cannot give: the long tail
  of shapes nobody has scanned, and the variation within a species that a
  single specimen cannot express.

**Screen:** a place's page shows what its colonies are made of, and which are
scans.

**Done:** a frame with the water taken away shows coral rather than lumps.

### Where A1 got to

**In.** `tools/scanned.py` reads a Smithsonian glb; `tools/scans` fetches more
of them from the Open Access API by growth form. Looe Key's reef is built by
`tools/reef-survey` off the USGS colony list, not by `tools/reef.py`, and that
is where the scan is wired: 21 prototypes where there were 15, six of them the
scan, **8,888 of 83,055 colonies**. Both paths carry it.

Three things the first attempt got wrong, all of them now recorded in the code
that was wrong:

  The simplifier kept the largest triangles. The largest triangles on a scan
  are the flat ones, so it kept a slab and threw away half the depth of the
  specimen. Vertex clustering instead, which is uniform.

  The specimen is a third museum plinth by height. Sat on its lowest point it
  rendered as a coral standing on a plinth. `where_the_ground_is` finds the
  step and buries everything under it.

  Fifteen prototypes were five shapes in three colours, because the geometry
  was grown once per kind and reused across the shades. Every prototype gets
  its own draw now.

**Fetched.** With a key in `SI_API_KEY`, `tools/scans` pulled **eleven
specimens over five growth forms**, CC0, scale in metres:

| form | specimens |
| --- | --- |
| massive | *Astraea (Orbicella) coronata*, *Porites lobata*, *Porites monticulosa* |
| brain | *Diploria labyrinthiformis*, *Pseudodiploria strigosa* |
| branching | *Pocillopora molokensis*, *Madrepora horrida*, *Sideropora mordax* |
| table | *Leptoseris cailletti*, *Gemmipora brassica* |
| encrusting | *Millepora alcicornis* |

**No gorgonians.** The Smithsonian has no CC0 3D scan of a sea fan or a sea
plume, and those are the silhouette of this reef. They stay procedural and the
record says why.

Three things the fetcher got wrong first, each now in the code that was wrong:

  **The same specimen came back under several genera** through synonymy, so
  USNM 58 was downloaded three times under three names. Deduplicated by the
  package it came from, not by the name it was given. A reef standing three
  copies of one museum piece is worse than one standing a blob, because it
  reads as variation.

  **Files were named from the genus that was searched for**, which turned
  *Madrepora horrida* into `acropora_madrepora`: an identification nobody made.
  Named from the museum's own title now, and the growth form lives in
  `scans.json` beside it rather than in the filename.

  **`found_in` returned one scan per form.** Three different Acropora on a reef
  is the point; one Acropora three times is not.

`scanned.found_in` now reads `scans.json` where there is one and falls back to
the genus in the filename for a specimen somebody put there by hand. 21 tests
in `tools/`, and the six that used to skip because they named a file that had
been renamed now find the specimen the way the code finds it.

## A2 · Materials that are not `UsdPreviewSurface`

Carried from r2 step 6, which is part done: roughness, self-emission and
subdivision are in, and the three things that matter most are not, because the
preview surface cannot express them.

  **Subsurface scattering** — translucent tissue over white aragonite, which
  is why living coral glows and bleached coral does not.
  **Fluorescence** — GFP-like proteins re-emitting green and orange under
  blue-shifted light, and most of why reef footage looks otherworldly.
  **Displacement** at corallite scale.

All three need an MDL material. That is the step.

**Done:** the same colony under the same light, before and after, and the
second one is alive.

### Where A2 got to

**`catalog/materials/coral_tissue.mdl`** is in and rendering. Translucent
tissue over white aragonite, a wet sheen, light through the thin parts, an
`alive` parameter that takes a colony from healthy to bare skeleton, and a
stand-in for fluorescence.

**`tools/corallite.py`** makes the surface at polyp scale, because there is no
CC0 photograph of one to tile: a jittered hexagonal packing of cups for the
mounding corals, meandering valleys for the brains, with corallite sizes from
the genera that build these reefs. Tileable, seeds wrapped across the eight
neighbouring copies, and the normals wrapped too. 13 tests. Projected triplanar
in world space, because the prototypes carry no texture coordinates and a blob
has no good unwrapping, and because world space means a corallite is 2.6 mm on
a colony of any size.

Three things it got wrong, each now in the code that was wrong:

  **`material()` has no `emission` parameter.** Emission belongs to
  `material_surface`. The compiler says "no overloaded version of function
  'material' matches calling parameters" and names the line, not the argument.

  **Diffuse transmission on a boulder is not translucency.** On a closed solid
  it is light going in and the bright sand behind coming out, and an olive
  colony rendered as a pale grey pebble — the bleached look this material
  exists to stop. It is per growth form now: nought for a boulder, which is
  thirty centimetres of aragonite, and a real value for a branch tip or the
  edge of a plate.

  **The subsurface term mixed the skeleton's colour in directly.** Light
  scattering a millimetre down under tissue brightens what comes back; it does
  not wash it towards white.

**And one more thing that was not the material.** The coral colours were the
pixel values off photographs of Looe Key, used directly as albedo. A photograph
of a reef through ten metres of water already contains the light that fell on
it, and handing it to a renderer as albedo lights it twice. `tools/make-site`
says exactly this about the ground and fixed it there in September; the coral
kept the raw numbers. Turf-grown rock was claiming to reflect 36%. It is
10-18%, and it is 70% of this reef.

**Left:** the colonies still read paler than the reef they are on, and that is
**not** the material. The preview surface rendered the same colony at 0.587
against an albedo of 0.26, so it predates all of this. Halving every albedo
moved the brightest two per cent of the frame from 0.72 to 0.59, which is a
real change and not enough: the light on an upward-facing surface is hot
against the light on ground seen at a grazing angle, which is the sun-and-dome
balance, and that is A5. Displacement at corallite scale is a bump and not
displacement, and says so.

**And a question this raises that is not a rendering question.** What the
survey says is on this reef:

| | share |
| --- | --- |
| **low** (rock *or* Siderastrea, benthos cannot tell) | **69.8%** |
| stony | 20.6% |
| sea fans and sea plumes | 8.8% |
| head | 0.8% |

Seven tenths of the colonies on Looe Key are things the detector could not
identify, and they are drawn as pale domes standing on the seabed. If most of
them are turf-grown rock — which CREMP's 4% stony cover says they must be —
then they are **part of the pavement** and should be drawn as pavement that
stands up, not as coral-coloured pebbles scattered on it. That is most of why
the frame reads as gravel with pebbles on it rather than as a reef, and it is
a decision about what the evidence supports, not a shader setting.

**Decided, 18 September: leave them as coral for now.** Put to the owner with
the recommendation to draw them as rock and the warning that doing so makes the
picture bleaker, because a reef at 4% cover looks like a reef at 4% cover.
The call was to keep them and revisit later. So this stands as a known
overstatement rather than a thing nobody noticed, and anything that cites a
Looe Key frame for cover has to come back here first.

## A3 · Ground that holds at a metre

Carried from r2 step 10, not started, and the most stubborn thing in the last
two days. Detail maps are tiled, grain is baked in, anisotropic filtering is
on, and the floor is still the flattest thing in frame at close range.

  Habitat-class detail sets, **height-blended** rather than mixed.
  Sand ripples as real displacement, oriented by the current.
  The satellite colour demoted to a low-frequency tint.

**Done:** a frame from a vehicle sitting on the bottom, on sand, nowhere near a
colony, that is worth looking at.

### Where A3 got to

The reason the floor was flat was not the detail maps. It was that **none of
the ground a place has a record of was ever drawn.** `tools/ground` has been
building Looe Key's real seabed since the second of September: the USGS
orthomosaic where the survey flew, habitat class from the satellite's pattern
elsewhere, and a 1 m heightfield with the survey's own DEM feathered into
NOAA's two. The renderer opened a 2 m mesh with a stock beach tiled over it.

Three things changed, and all three show:

  **The terrain is the 1 m field.** Two million triangles where there were half
  a million, and relief under five metres goes from 0.18 m to 0.52 m at p99.
  That is the spur-and-groove, and it is the survey's, not an invention.

  **`catalog/materials/coral_ground.mdl`**, because no material in OpenUSD can
  multiply a map of one particular seabed by a tiled photograph of a real
  surface, and a seabed needs both: the site map is 24 cm a texel and smears at
  close range, the tiled photograph is the same few metres of somebody else's
  beach everywhere. Multiplied, with the tile divided through by its own
  measured average, the tile carries variation and no colour and the site map
  carries colour and no detail. A place still carries a preview surface for
  anything that cannot read MDL.

  **Two surfaces, height-blended by habitat class.** `damp_beach_sand_02` in
  the grooves and sediment, `coral_ground_02` on the spurs and pavement, chosen
  by the polygons the survey drew. Blended by height, so at the edge of a sand
  patch the pavement shows through the gaps between the grains instead of the
  two fading through a grey that is neither. The seabed was `coast_sand_03`,
  which is a shingle bank in Normandy.

**Left:** the frame is still dim and brown where a carbonate reef is pale, and
that is exposure, which is A5. Sand ripples oriented by the current are not
done, though the sand now has ripples of its own.

## A4 · It moves, and something lives in it

Carried from r2 step 9, not started. Gorgonians and soft coral flexing in the
current that is already in the physics. Then fish: schooling, per-species
behaviour, and scatter-and-reform when a thruster comes past, which is the part
an ROV pilot recognises instantly.

Placed by measured density and habitat class, so a monitoring mission can be
**scored** on counting them rather than swimming past scenery.

**Done:** ten seconds where nothing is being demonstrated and it is still worth
watching.

### Where A4 got to

**Fish, as things that are there.** `coral/life.py`: eight behaviour groups,
their shares taken from the 2,440 observations recorded at Looe Key over 114
species of bony fish. Flocking, plus the three things a plain flock has not got
and a reef fish has — it holds station on a patch, it holds a depth, and it
scatters and reforms. What frightens it is the vehicle's actual thrust, because
a machine drifting past on a current is a log.

**And countable, which is the point.** `seen_from()` answers what a camera at a
place and a heading has in frame, so a monitoring mission can be scored on
counting rather than on having swum past scenery.

**Bodies** in `coral/fishform.py`: three plans, grown, because nobody publishes
CC0 scans of Caribbean reef fish the way the Smithsonian publishes its corals.
At three metres through this water a fish is a silhouette and a colour.

**Gorgonians bend** in the surge that was already being computed for the hull.
7,317 of them at Looe Key, each with its own phase so a field of them is not
one sheet.

Measured on the box: 1,272 fish over 431 schools, 39 ms a frame to swim them
and 16 ms to bend the gorgonians.

**Five bugs, and what each one is worth remembering for:**

  **`Gf.Vec3f(*row)` over a numpy array.** The binding wants Python floats and
  gets `numpy.float32`, and says "did not match C++ signature", which is a
  sentence about a type nobody wrote down. There is no `pxr` on a development
  machine, so *nothing* covered the code that writes into USD: 51 tests passed
  on a reef that silently had no fish in it. There is now a stand-in for the
  parts of `pxr` this uses that is strict about exactly that, and a test
  asserting the stand-in is strict — a stub more forgiving than the real thing
  passes when the real thing fails, which is how this got through.

  **The exception was being swallowed.** The scene built, Kit started, the
  sheet rendered, and the only sign was the absence of a line nobody was
  looking for. A dive that cannot draw its fish is still a dive; one that
  cannot say why is not worth running.

  **Indexing a USD array 7,317 times took longer than the tour.** And it was a
  regression: that line was a `list(...)` an hour earlier and was changed to
  hold the Vt array so the per-frame write would not rebuild 83,000
  quaternions. Both costs are real. Copy out of USD once, rebuild from the
  copy.

  **The sway was looking for the reef before the reef was in the stage.** It
  found nothing, said so, and never moved anything. Caught from the *ordering*
  of events in the log: `no_sway` printed before `coral_drawn`.

  **The shoal was built in its own frame and slid into place afterwards**, so
  every seabed lookup and every boundary measured from the origin while the
  fish were a hundred metres away.

**And one about method, which cost more than any of them.** `tools/look`
clears the old sheet on every failure path, and none of those paths run when it
is killed from outside with `timeout` — which is exactly what one does while
investigating a hang. Three separate diagnoses in one morning were made against
the previous run's output. It clears the sheet *before* the container starts
now: what is on disk is from this run, or there is nothing there.

## A5 · The rest of the light

Carried from r2 steps 2, 3 and 5, all part done.

  **Red must die faster than green.** The water model is spectral and the
  renderer's fog takes one distance, so a red vehicle at fifteen metres still
  photographs the same as a blue one. Needs a medium the renderer does not
  have, or the frame's own depth.
  **Caustics from the wave spectrum** that Phase 2 of the last plan built, and
  volumetric shafts.
  **A camera that meters the frame** rather than computing exposure from
  depth. This is why a lamp correct at six hundred metres blows out at six.
  **Backscatter and marine snow**, which is the defining look of ROV footage.

**Done:** no frame in the sheet is blown out or crushed, and the surface stops
being a bright ceiling with hard edges.

### Where A5 got to: the camera meters the frame

**`/rtx/post/tonemap/iso` does not exist. Neither does `cameraShutter`.**

carb takes any key you hand it, so writing to a name the renderer does not
have creates a setting nothing reads, silently. The exposure has been written
to those two names for a fortnight. A **thirty-two fold** change in "iso"
moved the picture by a thousandth of a stop, which is how it was finally
caught: pin the exposure, render, measure the frame, and the two frames are the
same. Every argument about the lamps in that fortnight was an argument against
a control that was not connected to anything.

The real names are `filmIso` and `exposureTime`. `CORAL_CITY_SETTINGS=1` now
prints the renderer's whole `/rtx` tree, and it has to be asked **from the
frame loop**: before the renderer starts, every key under `/rtx` is one this
code invented, which is exactly why the dump taken at dive open showed only our
own five made-up settings and looked fine.

On top of that, `coral/metering.py`: meter the frame, three or four rounds,
then stop. Not the renderer's automatic exposure, which tracks for ever and
brightens a dark scene until it looks normal — that destroys the thing a dive
is measuring. A diver meters on the way down and shoots, and the picture gets
darker as they go deeper, which is true. 16 tests, no renderer in them.

Three things it got wrong on the way, each one now in the code that was wrong:

  **`schedule_capture(ByteCapture(...))` delivered exactly one frame per run**
  and then stopped, with no error. Metering now goes through
  `capture_viewport_to_file`, which is the call the sheet itself is made with
  and is therefore known to work here.

  **Every budget was in wall-clock seconds**, and a stills tour over two
  million triangles gets four updates in forty seconds while the scene
  resolves. The meter timed out mid-first-round. It counts updates now. The
  same mistake in the other direction ate the budget once the tour was held
  and updates became free.

  **The highlight guard only applied once the frame was already clipping**,
  which is not a guard. A bright end at 0.78 was read as headroom, the
  exposure was raised for the mid-tones, and every view in the sheet came back
  with its top half-percent at pure white.

  **The meter read a bilinear copy of the frame.** It reads a smaller copy
  because it runs on the frame loop, and averaging four pixels into one throws
  the bright tail away: on one frame it reported a bright end of 0.85 where
  the number was 0.92. A percentile of an averaged image is not the percentile
  of the image, so the meter believed it had a third of a stop of headroom it
  did not have and every sheet came back hot. Point sampling now.

### Where A5 got to: the water

Four more settings that were never connected, and one piece of geometry, all
found by dumping the renderer's own tree from inside the frame loop.

  **`/rtx/fog/fogZup/enabled` was false.** The fog measures height along *y*
  and this platform is z-up, so every height setting in that block was being
  applied sideways. Two earlier rounds of work went into chasing a hard band
  across the frame with a control pointing the wrong way.

  **`fogDistance` and `fogStartDistance` are not settings this renderer has.**
  The real names are `fogStartDist` and `fogEndDist`, and they were left at the
  default **five kilometres**. So every frame this platform has made has had a
  seabed sharp to the horizon through water that absorbs green in seventeen
  metres. Same failure as `/rtx/post/tonemap/iso`: carb takes any key you give
  it and makes one nothing reads.

  **`VEIL_STRENGTH` was 0.12**, and it was tuned against that broken ramp,
  where near and far got the same wash so the only way to keep a close colony
  its own colour was to turn the whole thing off. With the ramp at three
  attenuation lengths it is 1.6, and this fog *adds* light rather than blending
  towards it, so it is a brightness and not a fraction.

  **The band was the colour of nothing.** The fog is applied per pixel from
  that pixel's depth, so a pixel with no geometry behind it gets none and comes
  back black.

  Painting the background the veiling colour helped and could never finish the
  job, and it took an afternoon to work out why: this fog *adds* veiling light
  without absorbing any, so fully-fogged geometry is always brighter than empty
  space by exactly the light that geometry reflects. **No single background
  colour can match both.**

  So the fix is not a colour, it is geometry. `_horizon` stands a wall of water
  round the site at 900 m, from below the deepest point up to the surface:
  behind the seabed, well inside the sea above it. Every ray lands on
  something, everything is fogged the same way, and the horizon is continuous
  because it is made of the same stuff as what is in front of it. Emissive
  rather than lit, because there is no light at nine hundred metres to light it
  with.

  Measured at Looe Key, the step between the band and the water above it went
  from **0.30 to 0.09** in green; at Thuwal Deep, where the seabed is flat and
  hides none of it, the band is gone entirely.

  **And the sea ended at a hundred metres.** The wave mesh was 200 m across on
  a site 1000 m across, so from the middle the sea ran out at 100 m and the
  seabed ran on to 500. The gap between the two is what the band actually was.
  Fine cells at the size of a wave out to 200 m, then cells growing by a third
  a step out to 2.8 km: 111,000 points, and the sea reaches past anything a
  dive can look at.

Across the five views of the sheet, nothing is blown out and nothing is
crushed: 0.0% pure white and 0.0% pure black in four of them, 0.1% white in the
fifth.

### The half of the medium the renderer cannot do

Water does two things to a picture: it **adds** the light of the column in
front of a surface, and it **takes** the surface's own light away. `/rtx/fog`
does the first and cannot do the second, at any setting. The ray-traced
volumetric effects want a volume prim this scene has no way to author.

So it was tried in the materials, where each surface could measure its own
distance from the camera and attenuate per channel — which would also have
given red dying faster than green, the other thing owed here. The material is
written, both materials carry it, and **it is held at `veil = 0` because the
distance is wrong.**

`state::position()` in this renderer is **object space**, and
`state::transform_point` through `coordinate_internal -> coordinate_world`
does not move it. The proof is in the renders rather than in a document: a
coral prototype is a fifth of a metre across, so its positions are tiny and it
came back unattenuated, while the seabed mesh spans a kilometre, so its
positions run to five hundred and it came back fully absorbed. What the shader
was measuring was distance from the middle of the site, which is why the
ground under the lens was the most veiled thing in the frame and the horizon
was the clearest.

**Four renders went into learning that, three of them wasted**, and the reason
is worth more than the finding: **a place carries its own copy of the
materials it is drawn with.** A material edited in the checkout does not reach
a place until the place is built again, so three renders compiled the same
hour-old shader and came back pixel-identical while the file on disk changed
under them. `tools/look` now refuses a place whose materials differ from the
checkout's, the way it already refuses a stale build — which is the guard that
caught the next one an hour later.

**Clipping at the water's reach was tried and is wrong.** Three attenuation
lengths does stop the seabed running to the horizon, and it cuts the sea with
it: above the seabed's cut edge the whole upper half of the frame becomes
background with a hard line where the surface stops. It traded a thin dark
strip for a third of the picture.

**And the ray-traced volumetric effects do something after all.** They were
switched off on the grounds that they expose no settings of their own without
a volume prim, and the frame got visibly worse in exactly that way. Switching
them back on shrank the band from a third of the picture to a strip. Whatever
they do with the light in the column, they do it. What found that was a `git
diff` against the commit that had made the better frame, not the settings
tree, which says nothing about them at all — and that is the lesson worth
keeping from this whole stretch: **when a picture gets worse, diff the commits,
do not reason about the renderer.**

**Left in A5:** the per-pixel distance, and with it real absorption and red
dying faster than green. Caustics from the wave spectrum. Backscatter and
marine snow.

## B2 · The three places whose cover nobody checked — *checked, and the answer is the other way round*

**This comes before any of them is made prettier.** Red Sea at 1.4 million
colonies a square kilometre, Kaneohe at half a million, Al Fahal at 44
thousand: three different densities, none of them measured, all of them
rendered as fact.

**The labelling was already done.** Every place says where its cover came from
and none claims to be measured: Al Fahal "chosen: derived from depth and
substrate, not from any survey of Al Fahal", Kaneohe citing the Hawaii DLNR
2014 baseline, Red Sea "chosen on purpose: composed rather than surveyed".

**The numbers were not, and measuring them turned the complaint upside down.**
Every reef here is planted to a *cover map* — `zonation.cover` draws how much
coral each patch of ground should carry — and then to a *colony ceiling*
somebody types on the command line. Nothing ever compared the two. It does now:

| | cover its map asks for | colonies needed | colonies given | short by |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **red-sea** | 38.4% | 1,222,623 | 1,400,000 | **paid** |
| **kaneohe** | 56.3% | 2,363,714 | 500,000 | 4.7× |
| **al-fahal** | 43.2% | 11,267,488 | 400,000 | **28×** |
| **thuwal-deep** | 3.2% | 7,294,474 | 20,000 | **365×** |

Red Sea — the composed place, the one the plan singled out as "a render of a
reef seventeen times too dense" — is the only one of the four that actually
delivers the cover its own map asks for. The other three are built at whatever
density the ceiling allowed, between five and three hundred and sixty-five
times short of what they claim.

**Nothing could see this until the cover denominator was fixed.** Cover was
being measured inside the stands the colonies are planted in, where it *is*
roughly what was asked for by construction — Al Fahal measured 44.2% against a
target of 43.2% and looked right. Over the habitat the colonies were supposed
to fill it is 2.4%.

**Al Fahal now has a cover citation.** Khalil, Bouwmeester and Berumen,
"Spatial variation in coral reef fish and benthic communities in the central
Saudi Arabian Red Sea", PeerJ 5:e3410, 2017 (doi 10.7717/peerj.3410): nine
reefs off Thuwal by line-intercept on 10 m transects at 2 m and 10 m, mean
coral cover from **8.35% (±3.3)** on inshore Abu Shosha to **30.70% (±3.7)**
on midshelf Al-Taweel. Al Fahal is one of the three midshelf reefs and had the
most hard coral genera of the nine, at 23. Its own per-reef figure is in
Figure 5 and Table S5 rather than the text, so what is cited is the range and
the fact that Al Fahal is midshelf — a specific number still needs the figure
read off.

That range is the thing to build to, and it is roughly *half* what the cover
map currently asks for. So the fix is not to raise the colony ceiling
twenty-eight fold: it is that **43.2% was never the right target for this
reef**, and the map should be asking for something in the twenties.

**And the author list was wrong.** It read "Roberts et al." for a fortnight;
nobody of that name is on the paper. The title and the DOI were right, which
is how it survived — a citation that resolves is not a citation that is
correct, and this one was only checked because somebody went to the paper for
a different number entirely.

### What `zonation.cover` was doing, and what it does now

Both symptoms above — the unpayable cover targets and Thuwal Deep's 19 km² of
reef habitat half a kilometre below the light — came from the same place.

**Flat ground was pavement.** `hard` read `0.88 + stands / 1.5`, on an
argument written in the comment above it: "flat ground is pavement, not half
sand". That is true standing *on a reef*, and these are not reefs, they are
kilometre boxes drawn around reefs. With that pedestal, `hard` averaged 0.85
on every site and fell below 0.2 on under one per cent of Al Fahal. Rock has
texture and sand does not, so it is rugosity and relief now, with no pedestal:
`hard` averages 0.31 on Al Fahal and the habitat falls from 98.4% of the site
to 63.4%.

Al Fahal is the case that makes it plain. **A quarter of that site is
perfectly level at exactly 21.6 m** — not seabed, but where Sentinel-2 stopped
being able to see, with everything past it clipped to the limit. Under the old
line that plateau was 88% hard and carried about 44% coral. It is the flattest
ground on the site and it is sand now, which is the least wrong thing to call
a number a sensor could not read.

**The light curve did not reach nothing.** It stopped at 60 m with 4%, and
`np.interp` clamps past the end of its table rather than continuing — so every
depth below 60 m came back 4% for ever, and Thuwal Deep was handed a coral
ceiling of 4% over five square kilometres. It goes to nought at 150 m now,
which is about where mesophotic reefs stop.

That correctly left the deep with no cover map at all, and the first build
after it returned a place with no reef: the planting divides by the map. What
lives at 550 m is azooxanthellate and attaches to rock, so below the light the
colonies are placed against the hard ground and the colony count is the
count the place was built with — a choice, which its `--cover-from` already
said in so many words. `want` itself is left saying nought, because that is
the light's answer and it is right.

**And it says when it has stopped discriminating.** A reef is a structure and
a site is a box drawn around it; an inference that calls two thirds of a box
reef habitat is not telling reef from sand. Kaneohe (52%) now passes, Red Sea
(92%) does not and says so.

| | habitat was | is | target | needed | given | delivers |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **al-fahal** | 98.4% | 63.4% | 24.2% | 4,022,803 | 400,000 | 3.8% |
| **kaneohe** | 94.8% | 52.4% | 40.8% | 898,772 | 500,000 | 34.8% |
| **red-sea** | 98.2% | 92.2% | 31.3% | 1,020,612 | 1,400,000 | 44.1% |
| **thuwal-deep** | 30.0% | 7.1% | — | — | 20,000 | 0.06% |

Kaneohe went from 4.7× short to 1.8× and now delivers 34.8%, just under the
37.9–88% its DLNR citation gives. Red Sea still pays for its own target.

**Owed:**

  **Al Fahal** — still ten times short, and now delivering 3.8% against a
  survey that puts midshelf Thuwal reefs between 8% and 31%. It is the one
  place that is now too *sparse* rather than too dense, and the fix is its
  colony ceiling: about 1.7 million for 15% cover, against Red Sea's 1.4
  million which builds fine. That changes how the reef sixty-seven of
  sixty-eight dives are flown over looks, so it is a decision and not a
  default.
  **Al Fahal's ground** — the real fix is the bathymetry. A quarter of it is a
  sensor floor. This is B3's ICESat-2 item and it is now the blocking one.
  **Kaneohe** — the DLNR range it cites is 37.9–88.0%, wide enough to be no
  constraint. The Allen Coral Atlas covers the bay and is a number.
  **Red Sea** — composed, so there is nothing to check it against, and that is
  the answer: its cover is whatever a person chose *on purpose*, and it says
  so. It is also the only place whose planting delivers what it chose. Its
  habitat is still 92% of its box, which for composed terrain may simply be
  true.
  **Two hardness functions.** `zonation.describe` decides what the coral
  grows on; `make-site.derived_hardness` decides what the ground is *painted*
  as. They are separate implementations of one idea and only one of them was
  fixed. This is the same shape as the two covers.

**Done:** every place's colony count traces to a survey or is labelled as
chosen, and no page shows an unsourced density as though it were measured —
*and* every place says whether the reef it built is the reef its map asked

## B5 · Thuwal Deep, a different world — *done, less the brine pools*

Six hundred metres: no sunlight, no coral, no algae, and none of Phase A's
library applies. Everything is seen by lamp.

**Done.** The `deep` assemblage — whips, glass sponges, crusts, and nothing
that needs light, cited to the Red Sea Decade Expedition. Holothurians, which
are not a coral and are the most conspicuous animal on a bathyal seabed.
Bioturbated mud instead of the wave-rippled sand every place was floored with,
with the trails, burrow mouths, mounds and casts that make it — and the
holothurians that made the trails. Marine snow, at the density Martin's curve
says is still falling at 550 m rather than the euphotic-zone count. And a
benthic transect, `missions/transect-thuwal-deep.json`, which asks the one
question a deep ROV pilot actually asks: how low to fly.

Four faults found by looking at its frames, all of them the same shape — a
near-field number applied globally, or a threshold set for a reef applied to a
place that is not one:

  - the veil had a floor, so 4% of a veil lit six kilometres of empty ocean
    and put a grey sky with a hard band across it over a place with no light;
  - the marine snow kept its half-metre clear of the *vehicle* and the still
    camera was elsewhere, so a fleck rendered as a boulder;
  - `best_ground` looked for reef between 8 and 18 m on a site that runs 519
    to 638, found none, and started every dive on empty mud;
  - and a build that died halfway left a place that was half new and half old
    and rendered without complaint, which `being-built.json` now refuses.

**Not done, and deliberately: the brine pools.** They are the Red Sea's
signature deep feature and they are not at this site. The brine deeps —
Atlantis II, Discovery, Kebrit, Shaban, Suakin — lie in the axial rift at a
thousand to two thousand metres. Thuwal Deep is continental slope at 519–638 m
and there is no brine pool documented on it. Building one here would be
inventing a feature and attributing it to a place, which is the one thing this
platform is built not to do. A brine pool is a *place*, not a feature to
sprinkle on this one: it belongs in a new site, on its own bathymetry, with
its own citation — and when there is one it is worth doing properly, because
the halocline is a mirror and a vehicle that crosses it is a vehicle whose
buoyancy changes under it.

**Still owed here.** The hard half of the deep ground is still
`coral_ground_02`, which is photographed *coral rubble* — the one texture that
cannot be right on a slope with no reef-building coral on it. And the
holothurians are placed by the same cover map as everything else, which
favours hard ground while most of them are out on the open mud between; that
needs a second placement channel for the things that want soft ground.

## B6 · The tank — *built and flyable*

Not a reef and not to be made one. It is the only place on this platform whose
geometry is *known* rather than derived, which makes it the home for the
physics validation in C3.

`tools/make-tank` builds one from three dimensions and a provenance string,
all four required — a tank whose size was chosen is a reef with extra steps,
and the record says "read, not fetched" in the same words `tools/cite` uses
about a paper. `mhl-tank` is the Michigan towing tank at 109.73 × 6.71 × 3.05
m, and it carries what a tow *is* so a mission need not re-derive it: half the
tank's depth, and the middle sixty per cent of the length, because a carriage
accelerates, holds and decelerates and only the held part is a measurement.

It flew on the first attempt and the log said what it had been given: a
nine-hundred-metre horizon wall round a hundred-and-ten-metre building, two
and a half kilometres of sea surface over it, and a JONSWAP field on that
surface with thirty centimetres of swell — a third of a hull's draught,
arriving in a concrete box from a fetch that does not exist. A place whose
whole purpose is to put a number on the physics cannot have that. `enclosed`
in the site record now takes the horizon away, stops the surface at the rim
with uniform cells, fixes it to the tank instead of to the vehicle, and makes
it flat. Asked of the place and never inferred from its size: Looe Key's
layout plot is smaller than this tank is long.

**Owed here:** the water is still Jerlov 1C, which is clear *coastal* water.
A towing tank is tap water and pure water's absorption is among the most
precisely measured quantities in optics (Pope and Fry 1997). That is a real
water type to add, and it is the one place on the platform where the medium
could be checked against a measurement rather than argued about.

# The band across the top of every frame — settled, 24 September

It is the fog. Not the horizon wall, not the sea surface, not the background.

This was worth two renders to settle and it had been guessed at four times.
`CORAL_CITY_PAINT_HORIZON=1` paints the wall magenta, the surface cyan and
opaque, and the background orange. With the water in, the frame came back
*identical* — and the first reading of that, the wrong one, is "the switch did
not arrive". It had: half a million pixels changed, by eight units. With
`CLEAR=1` on top, the whole grey field went black and neither paint appeared
anywhere. So there is no geometry there to paint, and the grey is the fog
alone, laid over water.

`/rtx/fog` adds veiling light and absorbs nothing, and it adds the same amount
at every distance past `fogEndsAtM`. So an empty sky is painted one flat grey,
and the hard line is simply where the depth behind it steps — the sea surface
in front of the wall, the wall beyond it. Both are fully fogged; they are
different greys because the fog is added to different amounts of nothing.

At Thuwal Deep this is now gone, because the veil follows the daylight to
nought and there is no fog left to paint anything with. In shallow water the
veil is legitimately high and the band is still there, and it cannot be fixed
by taking the fog away. It needs the fog's colour to *grade* with distance,
which is the same thing as saying the medium has to be the volumetric one
below rather than a screen-space wash. That is the work, and this is the
reason for it.

The diagnostic now says whether it painted (`horizon_made.painted`), because
telling "these pixels are not the wall" from "the switch never arrived" is the
whole question and the picture cannot answer it.

# The medium: why it cannot be done this way, and what to do instead

**Settled, 19 September. No per-pixel varying value reaches an MDL expression
in this renderer.** Both routes were painted into the frame and both came back
flat to three decimals across the whole seabed:

  `base::coordinate_source(...).position` — constant.
  `state::texture_coordinate(0)` — constant.

Texture *lookups* work: `base::file_texture` with that same coordinate lays
the colour map on correctly, which is exactly what made the route look sound.
A lookup and an arithmetic read are not the same thing here.

**That one fact is behind every dead end of the day.** `here_is` was a fixed
point, so the distance was a constant, so no camera position by any delivery
mechanism could ever have changed a pixel. Eight mechanisms were tried — USD
input per frame, USD input before compile, `uniform` dropped, plain floats
instead of a float3, the camera moved a hundred metres, the place's own MDL
default patched, a full rebuild — and each was blamed in turn for a silence
none of them caused.

**And the reason only one change all day moved the picture:** a compiled MDL is
reused until the place is rebuilt. `make-site` writes a fresh material and that
is what recompiles it. The red test worked because it happened to follow a
rebuild. Every render command that matters now runs `make-site` first.

**What to do instead, and it uses only what is proven to work.** The medium is
a function of distance from the camera, and a place's dive starts from a known
point. So compute it at build time and **bake it into the site colour map**:
for each texel, the distance to where dives begin, the per-channel survival
over that distance, and the veiling light added. One texture lookup then
carries the whole medium, and texture lookups are the thing that works.

It is the same approximation `tools/aim-water` already records — water measured
from where the dive starts, not from wherever the vehicle wandered — and it
should carry the same line in `site.json` saying so.

**The lesson, and it is the one this file keeps having to write down.** A frame
that does not change is evidence about the mechanism, not the value. Four
separate conclusions were drawn from identical frames and all four were the
same fact in different clothes. The check that would have found this on the
first afternoon — paint the input and see whether it varies — costs one render.

## C3 · It can be trusted — *the physics has a check; the deliverables do not*

**The physics held against something real — *done, as far as it can be
without a tank test to compare with*.** `tools/tow` runs the BlueROV2 down
`mhl-tank` and answers two questions that are not the same question.

*Does the simulator obey its own model?* Hold the hull at a speed — velocity
imposed, because a carriage does not negotiate with a hull, it drags it — and
read the pull. At steady state that must equal `D_l U + D_q U²` from the very
table it was integrated from. It does, to three parts in a thousand, at every
speed from 0.1 to 1.5 m/s. Released under a steady pull it also arrives at the
positive root of `qU² + lU − F` to five in ten thousand, which is the half
that tests the integrator and the effective mass — 17.00 kg along the tow,
11.5 of hull and 5.5 of water dragged with it.

*Are the coefficients the right size at all?* That cannot be settled by a
table against itself, so the towed drag is put beside what a bluff open frame
of the hull's published dimensions gives. Not ITTC-1957: that is flat-plate
friction for ship hulls, and against an open frame of cylinders it would be an
order of magnitude low and a false pass. A frontal area with a solid fraction
of 45–70% and a bluff Cd of 0.85–1.25, both stated as a band because a single
number would be a second unchecked table pretending to check the first. The
model lands inside it at every speed where a quadratic band is a fair thing to
hold it against — above 0.67 m/s, which is three times `l/q`, below which this
hull's drag is mostly linear and the comparison is of the wrong things.

That is not a calibration and it does not claim to be. It is the difference
between a table nobody has ever held against anything and a table that has
survived an independent order-of-magnitude check, with the band and its
reasoning written down for the next person to disagree with.

Two things fell out of building it. `dynamics.json` never said how big the
hull is, which is why no coefficient could ever have been checked; it does
now, with its provenance. And `hydrodynamics.py` had claimed since it was
written that fresh water and seawater differ by eight per cent. Twenty-seven
parts in nine hundred and ninety-eight is 2.7, and nothing caught it until the
tow printed the difference next to the sentence claiming it.

**Owed:** an actual tank measurement to compare against — the band is an
estimate, and a real carriage run on a real BlueROV2 would replace it with a
number.

**Something comes out of it — *done for a place; not yet for a dive*.**
`tools/deliver` turns a place into a colony inventory (`colonies.csv`), the
same points in WGS84 (`colonies.geojson`, which is what GeoJSON is defined to
be), a coverage raster as an ESRI ASCII grid with its projection beside it
(`cover.asc`, `cover.prj` — plain text, no GDAL, opens in QGIS and numpy
alike), cover broken down by growth form, and `provenance.json`.

The georeference is the exact inverse of the expression each place was sampled
with, written out in words in `provenance.json` so it can be checked without
reading the code that performed it. `deliver` keeps its own copy of
`metres_per_degree` deliberately: a transform whose two halves share an
implementation cannot be checked by comparing them, and a test does compare
them.

`provenance.json` is the file that matters. Every column says whether anybody
measured it, and the answer differs by place and by column: at Looe Key the
positions and diameters *are* observations — a detector found each colony in
the SQUID-5 DEM — while the growth form assigned to it is not, and on a grown
reef none of it is. Cover is quoted over the ground that was surveyed rather
than over the site, because seven eighths of Looe Key's square kilometre has
no colonies for want of anybody having flown it, and quoting the site average
would put the reef at a fifth of what its own transects say.

Which produced the first independent cross-check this platform has had.
Looe Key's delivered cover, summed from 83,055 detections over the 107,950 m²
the survey actually flew, is **4.28% of that ground standing** — 2.67% "low",
0.84% "stony", 0.23% "head", and half a per cent of gorgonians. CREMP's
transects put stony coral cover at 3.4–4%. Two surveys, two methods, and they
agree to within the question of whether the detector's "low" class is stony
coral — which is a question about a classifier and not about either number.
It is written into `provenance.json` as a comparison to make, not as a claim.

Building it turned up the thing that made it necessary. The first version
worked the growth forms and footprints out *of the USD*: it assumed the
prototype index was kind × variants + variant and read the names off
`reef.kinds`, which on a surveyed place is a histogram of what the survey
counted and not a list of forms. Every row came out labelled "low" or
"unknown" and every footprint was a hundred times too small — and it showed up
as a reef with no cover on it rather than as an error. Both builders now write
down what each prototype is and how a colony's scale should be read, and
`deliver` refuses a place that does not say.

**Owed:** this is a place, not a dive. Outplant positions from a *flown
mission* — what the vehicle actually did, where it actually put things — is
the other half, and it needs the layout and the run record rather than the
reef.

**Missions that are plans of work.** Three of the four on the platform are
parametric or are test fixtures.

# How this one is kept honest

**Judged on five, not on one.** Every Phase A step is checked with
`tools/look` across all five places in the same run. A change that improves
Looe Key and ruins Kaneohe is not an improvement, and the last plan had no way
of noticing.

**Evidence before polish.** B2 comes before anything in Phase B is made
prettier, because a beautiful render of a reef seventeen times too dense is
worse than an ugly one — it is more likely to be believed.

**The instrument is not optional.** Four bugs in the test rig cost more of the
last two days than the rendering did: a stale image, an empty environment
variable, a light placed out of frame, and frames crossing between places.
Every frame now carries the build and the place that made it and both are
checked before it is kept. That machinery stays and gets extended, not
bypassed.

**Run the product's tests, not only the platform's.** The SDK's suite had been
failing for at least a hundred commits and nobody had run it. The thing an
outside developer touches first is the thing least tested.

**What would show this plan is wrong:** the USGS fetch in B1 turning out to be
unusable at the scale it claims; the cover checks in B2 finding no survey
exists for Kaneohe or Al Fahal, which would make three of five places
permanently uncitable; or all of Phase A landing and a reef scientist still
picking the render out of a line-up immediately.

## What a frame is evidence of

Four separate failures this session produced the same symptom — a frame that
did not change — and each one was read, at least once, as evidence that the
change made no difference. In order, and every one of them silent:

1. `tools/aim-water` patched the place's own copy of `coral_ground.mdl`, which
   left it differing from the checkout, which is the condition `tools/look`
   refuses to render on. It printed the refusal and exited nought, the caller
   swallowed the output, and the sheet on disk stayed as it was. Four readings
   came off one frame.
2. `tools/gpu sync` ran `git diff --cached --check` before committing, and a
   directory of third-party CAD tripped it on trailing whitespace. Three
   commits in a row did not happen. The output was long enough that the reason
   scrolled past, and it was piped to `tail -1`.
3. `tools/look` pulls a prebuilt image. The place is mounted, so a material or
   a mesh reaches the next frame; the runtime is not, and only a full
   `tools/box up` rebuilt it. Every camera, metering and water change for a
   fortnight rendered against old code.
4. `base::file_texture` has no `gamma` parameter. Asking for one does not
   warn: the MDL module fails to compile, Kit substitutes a fallback material
   and renders a perfectly good picture of the wrong thing. The seabed came
   back flat red. The compiler had said so, in the log, all along.

Each is now a guard rather than a lesson. `tools/look` refuses a stale
runtime, refuses a place whose material has fallen behind, fails the run when
a place refuses, and reads the MDL compiler's own errors out of the log before
it keeps the frames. `.gitattributes` marks the CAD binary. The image carries
the tree hash of the runtime inside it, and `tools/box sim` builds just that.

The thing they have in common is worth naming: **a tool that cannot do its job
must fail, not report.** Every one of these printed the truth somewhere and
carried on, and carrying on is what made the output indistinguishable from
success.

And its corollary, which cost the most: **when a change produces no visible
difference, the first hypothesis is that the change did not arrive.** Not that
it did nothing.

## The camera, which was the missing half of the medium

The medium was right and every frame was green, and those two things were not
in conflict. Green is what an instrument records pointed into coastal water.
Every underwater photograph anybody has seen has been through a camera that
adapted to it, and a render that skips the adaptation does not look like
footage — it looks like data.

So the camera now does both halves on its own frame: meters the light, and
measures the cast. Both are logged (`metered`, `camera_balances`), so a sheet
says what the camera did to it.

Three attempts, and the two that failed are worth keeping:

- **A grey card at three metres**, worked out from the attenuation lengths.
  It asked for gains of 0.99, 1.00, 1.00 — nothing — and the reason is not
  obvious: within a few metres the water's own backscatter puts about as much
  into red as absorption takes out, so a card at arm's length really is
  neutral. The cast is not in the near field. It is in the other forty metres
  of the frame. Measured off the frame, the way the exposure beside it is.
- **`/rtx/post/colorcorr/gain`**, which is a real setting, read out of the
  renderer's own tree rather than guessed. It takes the numbers and applies
  something else to them: a red gain of 1.138 turned a green reef magenta,
  and pinning that exact triple reproduced it to two decimal places. So the
  setting works, and its meaning is written down nowhere. Enabling the stage
  at unity is pixel-identical to leaving it off, which is how we know it was
  the gain and not the stage.
- **Waiting for the capture inside the frame loop**, to balance it as it was
  written. Kit writes a capture on a later update than the one that asks for
  it, so the wait was waiting for work that could not start until the wait
  ended. It gave up politely and the frames came out untouched.

It lives in `tools/balance-sheet` now, on the finished sheet, from the gains
the runtime logged — sRGB decoded, scaled, re-encoded, because multiplying an
encoded value is not multiplying the light it stands for.

## The water every place is in

All five places are rendered through Jerlov 1C. That is coastal water, and it
is the default in `tools/look`, the default in `tools/aim-water`, and the
suffix on every sheet this platform has ever produced.

It is a fair description of Looe Key, in the Florida Keys. It is not one of Al
Fahal, which is an offshore Red Sea reef where a diver sees thirty or forty
metres — rendered through 1C it comes back as fog, and the frame is a correct
picture of the wrong sea. Kaneohe Bay is the other way: a genuinely turbid
embayment that 1C probably flatters.

`tools/aim-water` now bakes whatever water a place has recorded and falls back
to 1C. No place records one yet, and none should be given one here: a water
type is a claim about a place, and it wants a source the way its density does
— which is the whole point of `--cover-from` and of the density table in
`tools/check-places`. The work is to find, per place, a measured attenuation
or Secchi depth and the survey it came from, and to write that in beside the
rest of the record.

Until then every sheet is labelled `-1C` and that label is honest.

## Al Fahal, and three bugs that all looked like one

Al Fahal was the place that would not cue. Its seabed held a tenth of its own
colour at arm's length and a tenth at the horizon — flat — where Looe Key held
a quarter falling to a thirtieth. Distant colonies came out *brighter* than
near ones, which is the signature of a medium pointing the wrong way.

It was not one fault and none of them was the medium.

**The veil did not know what water it was in.** `VEIL_BRIGHTNESS` was 0.62 for
every Jerlov type, so the clearest open ocean backscattered exactly as much
light as a turbid harbour and a water type only ever changed the colour of the
veil, never how much of it there was. The veil is backscattered light, and how
much comes back is precisely what a Jerlov number is a statement about. Deep
ocean water is usually taken to return about five per cent of what falls on
it against twenty-five or more for turbid water, so it now scales off the
green length, anchored on 1C where the 0.62 was read off a ladder. About a
fourfold spread across the table. Al Fahal's water went from 0.62 to 0.45.

**A reflectance is not an sRGB pixel.** `derived_colour` builds the ground for
every place without a survey, out of measured reflectances — 0.74 for
carbonate sand, 0.20 for rock under turf — and wrote those numbers straight
into a texture the renderer reads as sRGB. Decoded, 0.20 comes back 0.033: six
times darker than the rock it says it is drawing. Al Fahal's seabed was at a
third the brightness of Looe Key's, and a dark seabed under a bright veil is a
washed-out seabed. Looe Key was never wrong because its ground is an
orthomosaic — a photograph, which genuinely is sRGB.

That one is worth the space because the function's own docstring warns against
the neighbouring mistake — "a picture of a reef through ten metres of water is
not the reef's albedo, and this platform has made that mistake once already" —
and it was still writing linear numbers into an encoded file.

**A coral material kept the thousand metres it was compiled with.** The map
lookup turns a world position into the map's own square and needs the site
width to do it. The runtime knows the width and writes it into every material
that *has* that input — and the coral's USD never declared one, so it kept the
MDL default. Looe Key is a thousand metres across and was right by
coincidence. Al Fahal is three thousand, so every colony read the map a long
way outside its edges and came back the colour of the clamp, which is black.

Three faults, one symptom, and the only way through was to stop looking at the
frame and start measuring the things behind it: the colour map's own mean, the
veil in the log, the width in the USD.

## Red Sea, and a metric that was reading the wrong thing

Red Sea was reported as the one place whose ground would not cue: 0.174 of its
own colour near, 0.146 far, where the others fall to a twentieth.

There is no ground. At 1.4 million colonies a square kilometre the coral
covers the seabed completely, and the band of pixels the metric calls "the
ground near the camera" is a staghorn thicket. Its colonies cue at 0.54, which
is Kaneohe's 0.50 and better than Looe Key's 0.69. The medium was working the
whole time and the number was measuring coral.

Worth writing down because the metric is the one used to judge every place on
this platform, and on four of five it happens to be reading what it says it is.

## The band across the top

Separately, and in every place rather than this one: a flat two-tone band
between the reef and the surface. What was established, and what was not.

Rendered with the medium off it goes to 0.06 — black. So it is empty space
being filled by the fog and the background, which is exactly what the note
above `HORIZON_AT_LEAST_M` says the wall was built to prevent.

The wall is built. It logs itself now: radius 900 m, top at the water line,
bottom 88 m down, 192 points, visible. Painted a colour nothing else in the
scene is, it shows up in about 180 pixels at the very bottom edge of the frame
and nowhere near the band. The sea surface is built too — 110,889 points out
to 2,500 m — and painting it opaque put none of that colour in the band
either. Nor did painting the background, nor the dome.

So the band is empty space, the wall exists to fill empty space, and the wall
is not filling it. That is as far as this got.

Two things to be careful of next time round. The band has two tones and they
are not the same fault: the upper is close to the water's own colour (R/G 0.85
against the veiling's 0.827) and the lower is too neutral (0.94). And four
"eliminations" in a row were nearly thrown away on a misread grep — the paint
switch looked unplumbed because a docker pull had pushed the answer off the
top of a `tail`. A frame that does not change is evidence about the mechanism,
not the value, and so is a grep that prints nothing.

### What it was

Two faults, and the first one explains why five years of staring at the frame
never found it.

**The wall could not reach.** It stood from the seabed up to the water line,
and the water line is about seven metres above a camera doing reef work. At
nine hundred metres out that is half a degree. The wall could only ever cover
the half-degree of frame just above eye level; everything higher went through
the sea surface — which is transmissive, as it has to be for Snell's window —
and out of the water, where there is no geometry at all. Those rays came back
the background colour. A wall built so that "no pixel in the frame is looking
at nothing" was covering less than one per cent of the pixels that were.

It now stands to `water_level + radius`, which is forty-five degrees from
where a camera sits. Above the water line it is not a wall of water any more;
it is the horizon, and it is the same colour because it is the same light.

**The wall was the veiling colour twice.** It was emissive at
`veiling * veil`, on the reasoning that nothing lights a surface nine hundred
metres down. True, and it did not matter while the wall was invisible. Once it
filled the top of the frame it mattered a great deal, because this fog *adds*
veiling light without absorbing any — the note above `HORIZON_AT_LEAST_M` says
so — and an emissive wall therefore comes back at the veiling colour twice
over, brighter than any real geometry at the same distance. The distant reef,
which is fully fogged and reflects almost nothing, lost a third of its depth
cue to a backdrop that out-shone it.

Black, now. A black wall at nine hundred metres is exactly "geometry, fully
fogged, reflecting nothing", which is what the far seabed already is, so the
two sides of the horizon are the same calculation.

Measured: the band above the reef went from R/G 1.03 — neutral, the colour of
nothing — to 0.91–0.94, which is the water. Not all the way to the veiling's
own 0.83, and that gap is the next thing.

### What is left

A brightness step at the horizon line, with a flat featureless region above
it. That line is the sea surface seen edge-on, which is a real feature and
sits higher at Red Sea because that camera looks down the fore-reef slope and
so pitches further down. What is above it has no waves, no caustics and no
Snell's window in it: a plain backdrop rather than the underside of a sea.
That is a simplification, not a wrong colour, and it is the honest description
of where this got to.

### On the metric, again

The colony depth-cue figure got *worse* on three places after this fix, from
about 0.5 to about 0.85. It is not a regression. The far field was the
background colour, which was darker than the water it stood for; it is now
the water. Distant water really is brighter than a shadowed near reef. That is
the second time in two days this metric has reported a fault that was not
there — the first was Red Sea's ground, which is not visible at all.

## The metric, which was the thing reporting the faults

Twice in two days a number said a place was broken when it was not, and both
times the number was believed for a while. It was worth fixing the number.

There was never a tool. The figure was re-improvised inline each time it was
wanted, and every version of it guessed distance from image rows: the bottom
of the frame is near, the middle is far. That is wrong in three ways that all
bit.

- **Red Sea has no visible ground.** At 1.4 million colonies a square
  kilometre the coral covers the seabed, so "the ground near the camera" was
  a staghorn thicket and the medium was reported broken while working.
- **The horizon is not at a fixed row.** Row 164 at Red Sea, where the camera
  looks down the fore-reef slope; row 322 at Al Fahal. A fixed far band is
  sky in one place and seabed in another.
- **A real fix read as a regression.** After the horizon band was mended the
  figure got worse on three places, because the far field stopped being the
  background colour and started being the water, which is brighter.

`tools/cue` does not guess. It rebuilds the camera from the same `VIEWS`
table the runtime flies, the place's own `beginAt`, and the lens written down
in the extension — then traces every pixel against the place's heightfield
until it meets the seabed. What it reports is colour against **metres**, with
the share of the frame that is seabed at all.

The first thing it said was that no view has a band inside eight metres.
Three metres up, a twenty-seven degree frame pitched two degrees down: the
nearest ground a camera can see is about ten metres away. Every "near" band
ever quoted on this platform was ten to thirty metres of water.

### Telling a pale reef from a failed medium

Saturation is albedo and medium together and the two do not separate by
looking. Red Sea keeps less colour at 8–16 m than at 32–64 m, which reads as
a broken medium and is a pale cream staghorn foreground.

So `--against` takes a sheet of the same flight with the medium off and
divides one by the other, normalised on the nearest band. What is left is the
water. On Red Sea:

    8-16   1.000  1.000  1.000
    16-32  0.919  0.976  1.002
    32-64  0.847  0.916  1.039
    64-128 1.059  1.085  1.290

Out to sixty metres red goes fastest and blue slowest, which is what Jerlov
IB says. Past that the ratio climbs over one, because the veil adds light
where the frame with the water off has none. Both halves right, and Red Sea
confirmed sound a second time — this time with the reef divided out.

### And the tests nobody ran

`just test` ran Go and the workspaces. Four hundred Python tests sat in
`services/sim-runtime` and `tools` and no recipe touched them; they were run
by hand, which means they were run only when somebody already suspected
something. `just test-python` now runs them — from inside `coral`, because
several import their neighbours as top-level modules and from the root
`test_hydrodynamics` cannot find `hydrodynamics` and collection stops there.

## Is the veil too strong? No.

Standing question since the medium started working: with the water off the
reef is mustard and ochre, with it on the whole frame is teal. Too much veil,
or the water being right?

`tools/cue --against` answers it. Every pixel of a distance band is the same
water over a different surface, so a least-squares line through (medium off,
medium on) gives both terms at once — the slope is what the surface sends that
survives, the intercept is what the column in front of it glows.

Measured on Looe Key, both frames rendered at a pinned ISO 400 with the meter
and the white balance switched off, so the only difference between them is the
water:

    metres    transmission        Jerlov 1C says
     8-16     0.04 0.48 0.40      0.05 0.49 0.40
    16-32     0.00 0.33 0.24      0.00 0.24 0.16

The medium transmits what the water type says, to two decimal places at close
range. The veil it adds is 0.04 near and 0.09 by a hundred metres — modest and
monotonic. At twelve metres of coastal water red is down to four per cent; a
teal frame is the correct picture of that, and the mustard reef is what a lamp
at arm's length would show, not what a camera twelve metres off sees in
daylight.

So: not a fault, and the medium is now verified end to end against the physics
it claims — baked map, per channel, right magnitude.

### Red Sea disagreed, and it was the measurement

The same fit on Red Sea reads a green length of forty to seventy metres where
IB says twenty-seven. That is the bias written into `tools/cue`'s own
docstring: the trace stops at the heightfield, coral stands two metres above
it, and at 1.4 million colonies a square kilometre every pixel is coral. The
distance is over-stated, so the transmission attributed to a band is one
measured much nearer, and the curve flattens.

Which is why the question was settled on Looe Key: four per cent cover, bare
ground, and a trace that lands on what the camera is actually looking at.
Choosing where to measure is part of the measurement.

### What the guard caught on the way

Two renders of this experiment were refused, and both sheets read as the run
before them. `just test-python` runs `uv` inside `services/sim-runtime`, where
there is a pyproject — so uv installed the package and left an egg-info and a
lock, `git add -A` committed them, and the tree hash of the runtime moved
although not one line of its code had. `tools/look` correctly refused to
render against an image that no longer matched the checkout.

The recipe passes `--no-project` now and the artefacts are ignored. The guard
did exactly what it was built for: the alternative was concluding something
about the veil from two identical files, which is how the last three days
went before the guards existed.

## A5, closed

The four bullets, and where each got to.

**Red must die faster than green.** Done, and now measured rather than
asserted. `tools/cue --against` fits `wet = dry x transmission + veil` across
a distance band, and at eight to sixteen metres Looe Key transmits
**0.04 / 0.47 / 0.36** where Jerlov 1C says **0.05 / 0.49 / 0.40**. Three
channels, within a few per cent.

**A camera that meters the frame.** Done earlier: exposure and white balance
read off the frame rather than computed from depth.

**Caustics from the wave spectrum.** Done. They were a painted texture —
the same net in a flat calm and a metre of swell. They now come from the sea
the dive is actually running. Four things had to be right and three of them
were wrong first:

  - *The band.* `SeaState` samples around the spectral peak, where the energy
    is, because energy is what moves a hull. An eight-second swell is a
    hundred and sixty metres long with a slope under one per cent and at six
    metres down it displaces a ray by a centimetre and a half. It focuses
    nothing. The net is thrown by the chop.
  - *The method.* Solving the refraction map's Jacobian is single-valued, so
    it can only sharpen with depth — it said a reef at sixty metres wore a
    harder net than one at three. Counting where rays land gets the crossings
    for free.
  - *The sun is not a point.* Half a degree wide blurs a caustic across
    `depth x tan(a quarter degree)`: fourteen millimetres at three metres,
    twenty-eight centimetres at sixty against a fifteen-centimetre chop. That
    is why caustics are a shallow-water sight. Applied as per-ray jitter it
    measured its own shot noise, so it is a blur on the light that landed.
  - *The slope, not the height.* Scaling the optical band to a share of the
    significant height was a guess and it made a surface far too smooth to
    focus anything. Cox and Munk read the sea's mean square slope off the
    sun's glitter from an aircraft in 1954 — 0.003 + 0.00512 U — and that is
    what the band is scaled to now. It is the slope that bends a ray.

  And one more, which is why the first nets came out as vertical streaks
  rather than a mesh: the chop runs every way at once. Taking the swell's
  thirty-degree spread for the optical band made every component nearly
  parallel, so every ray displaced along one axis. A net needs rays folding
  across each other from two directions.

  It is laid on by the *material*, not projected by the caustic light. A
  ninety-metre emitter five metres above the floor cannot cast a feature a
  third of a metre across, because every point of the floor sees the whole of
  it. Measured with the net on the light: a rough sea came back with *less*
  fine contrast than a flat calm, at every scale.

**Backscatter and marine snow.** Done. Real geometry in the water, lit by the
same lamps as the reef, drifting and sinking and staying where it is while the
vehicle goes through it — which is what separates backscatter from a decal on
the lens. The count comes off a measurement: five hundred to thirteen thousand
aggregates above half a millimetre in a cubic metre of oligotrophic water
(Pilskaln et al. 2005). Only the tail above two millimetres is drawn, about a
sixtieth of them; the rest are the veil, and drawing both would be counting
the same particles twice.

### And the shafts, which are further than expected

The renderer has a participating medium. `/rtx/raytracing/globalVolumetricEffects`
with an inscattering model that asks for exactly what a water type is made of
— a transmittance colour over a stated distance, a single-scattering albedo,
a phase anisotropy. It is configured from the Jerlov lengths and it works:
`VOLUME=1` puts light with structure through the water column instead of a
flat haze, which is the thing a global fog cannot do at all.

It is **off by default**, and that is a decision. A volume attenuates every
path, including the sun's way down — which is right, and is a job `light_for`
is already doing by dimming the sun with depth. Run together they take the
downwelling light twice: transmission at ten metres came back a tenth where
1C says a half, and the depth cue went flat.

Adopting it means handing the whole medium over: the sun stops being dimmed
by depth, the materials stop adding the veil, and the survival map is not
needed at all, because the volume works from each pixel's own distance rather
than from one baked point — which would retire the "measured from where the
dive begins" approximation that has been on the record since the medium was
written. That is a better platform. It is a change to the medium, not to the
look, and it wants its own pass with `tools/cue` to land it.

## B3 · Al Fahal, drawn as the reef it is

Sixty-seven of the sixty-eight dives on this platform happen at Al Fahal, and
it had the least behind it of any place: satellite bathymetry, and an
iNaturalist fetch that returned two hundred and seventy-eight fish
observations and **not one coral**. Nothing about what actually grows there.

**A reference of its own.** There is no API for what is known about this reef;
it is in a paper. `tools/cite` puts one into a place's reference the way a
download goes in — its own file, its own entry beside the fetched ones, the
citation with it, and `"fetched": false`, so that nobody later mistakes a
reading for a measurement of our own. Al Fahal now carries the PeerJ 5:e3410
line-intercept survey: nine Thuwal reefs at 2 m and 10 m, twenty-five
scleractinian genera, Al Fahal a midshelf reef, with Porites, Pocillopora and
Acropora each above five per cent of benthic cover — and the 2024 per-genus
split of coral cover, 34.6 / 22.8 / 6.4, recorded as the different quantity it
is.

**Grown as the ocean it is in.** The coral palette is morphological — massive,
branching, table, encrusting, plume, fan — and the *mix* was read off
Caribbean and Red Sea surveys together and used everywhere. That mix is heavy
in gorgonian fans at depth, and a gorgonian sea fan is a Caribbean signature.
The Red Sea has very few. What it has is Acropora tables on the slope and soft
corals, which are a different shape entirely.

So Al Fahal was being drawn as a Caribbean reef, in every frame, in the place
where nearly every dive happens.

`zonation.ASSEMBLAGES` now holds two mixes and a place says which it is grown
from, with the source, the same way it says where its density and its sea come
from. The Red Sea mix follows the genera: Porites massive and most abundant,
Pocillopora branching, Acropora table on the slope and branching in the
shallows, and fans down to a per cent or two. `check-places` prints which
ocean each reef was grown as and flags the ones nobody has said.

Kaneohe and Looe Key are still flagged. Looe Key is Caribbean and is right by
accident; Kaneohe is Hawaiian and is not. That is B4.

**And one thing the rebuild caught.** The seabed came out a third darker than
it should. `as_texture` normalised the caustic net's mean and *then* clipped
it to what eight bits can hold — so the bright tail went over the side and
what was left had a mean below a half, which multiplied the whole ground by
less than one. A caustic moves light about; it does not remove any. Clipped
first, then normalised: the frame went from 0.239 back to 0.415.

## B4 · Kāne'ohe, drawn as Hawaii

`Porites compressa` and `Montipora capitata` are the two most abundant species
in the bay, with `Porites lobata` the massive third — finger coral, rice
coral, lobe coral. Cover is high: the 2014 mitigation-bank baseline put it
between 37.9 and 88.0 per cent across the bay's reefs, 50.9 to 67.9 on the
controls. That is cited into the reference and the cover is now a number the
colony count can be held against rather than a number with nothing beside it.

The shape that matters is the one that is **absent**. The main Hawaiian
islands have essentially no Acropora, so there are no tables at all — the
thing a Red Sea slope is known for does not occur here. Nor are there shallow
gorgonian fans. An archipelago four thousand kilometres from anywhere has a
short list, and a reef drawn from a general idea of a reef gets it wrong in
the way a diver notices first.

`check-places` now prints which ocean each reef was grown as. And it no longer
asks the question of Looe Key: a surveyed reef's colonies were found in a
one-centimetre DEM and classified by shape, so there is no mix behind them,
and reporting it as an unlabelled one said the place was missing something it
cannot have.

## B5 · Thuwal Deep, which had never been flown

Every sheet of Thuwal Deep was a dark rectangle, and it was not a rendering
fault. **`beginAt` was null.** Every other place takes its dive start from its
coral — the best reef in the working band — and this place has none, because
it is five hundred metres down, which is the point of it. So nothing ever
chose one, and the consequences were quiet and total: no anchor for the
stills, so no camera; no camera, so no medium to bake; and no picture.

A place that cannot be flown is not a place. Ground with nothing growing on it
now takes its start from the ground: flat, at the depth most of the site is
at, inside the middle half so a vehicle can fly any way from it. Thuwal Deep
begins at 556 m and has a seabed in its frames for the first time.

**And then it showed up two numbers that were wrong everywhere.**

`is_it_deep` had a floor of two per cent on the daylight. Two per cent of the
sun is not a small number, it is a different scene: with an auto-exposure
behind it, the first frame this place ever made was a *daylit* seabed half a
kilometre below the last of the light. The floor is gone. Every caller that
divides by it already guarded, and a light of intensity nought is a light that
is off, which is what the sun is down there.

The veil had a floor too — four tenths, regardless of depth. The veil is
ambient light scattered back, and where there is no ambient light there is
nothing to scatter. It follows the daylight down now, with a floor only big
enough to stand for the water a lamp lights in front of its own lens. At 556 m
the camera opens to ISO 2800 and the frame is dark, which is correct.

**What is owed.** Still: bioturbated sediment with tracks and burrows, whip
corals, glass sponges, holothurians, brine pools. And one thing found on the
way that is worth more than any of them — **there is no lamp in the frame.**
The catalogue's BlueROV2 declares two fifteen-hundred-lumen Lumen Subsea
lights, and at ISO 2800 they would be blinding, and the deepest place on the
platform has no pool of light in it at all. At 556 m the lamps are the only
light there is, so whatever is wrong there is the first thing to fix before
anything is put on that seabed to be seen.

## The lamps, which had never been on

Thuwal Deep is five hundred and fifty metres down, where the lamps are the
only light there is, and that is why it found this. Three faults, stacked, and
each one hid the next.

**The vehicle had no lamps.** `tools/look` flew whichever package happened to
be newest in the work directory, and that package was published before the
vehicle had lights — its `dynamics.json` has no `lights` key at all. Every
sheet this platform has produced, at every place, was flown by a vehicle
carrying no lamps. In daylight nobody noticed.

This tool already refuses a runtime older than the checkout and a place whose
materials have fallen behind it. The vehicle was the third thing it draws with
and the only one not held to that rule. It flies the catalogue now, and says
what it is carrying before it starts, because a dark frame and a frame with
the lamps unshipped look the same.

**The lamps pointed backwards.** A rect light faces its own -Z. Aiming one was
a swing about Z and a tip about Y, and the tip was `90 + asin(z)` — which for
a lamp aimed level sends -Z round to *minus* X. And the swing was applied
before the tip, where it acts on a vector still pointing straight down and
does nothing at all, so every lamp came out aimed the same way whatever
bearing it was given.

For a lamp aimed straight down the two faults cancel exactly. That is why the
caustics — a rect light pointed at the seabed — have worked since they were
written, while the lamps never have, and why the day recorded in the comments
above them was spent on brightness and parenting and cone angles. It was
none of those.

It is one matrix now, from a rotation that takes -Z to the aim, with no order
and no sign left to get wrong, and checked against the seven directions that
matter.

**And the brightness was tuned against the fault.** `LAMP_SCALE` was seventy
thousand, which made a fifteen-hundred-lumen lamp render at a hundred and five
million — because it had been raised until a light that was facing away
showed up at all. The comment above it said, in as many words, that when the
camera metered properly this would become a real photometric conversion. The
camera does now. A Lambertian emitter of area A has luminance `flux / (A x
pi)`, so a Lumen Subsea across eight centimetres is seventy-four thousand
candela a square metre — four orders of magnitude below what it was.

**One more, found on the way out.** `lamps_made` logged the intensity by
working it out again from `1500 * LAMP_SCALE` rather than reporting what the
light was given, so the first frame after the photometric change reported a
hundred and five million while the scene held seventy-four thousand. A
readback that recomputes is not a readback.

Every place was re-rendered. The depth cue held and improved — Looe Key 0.375
near to 0.099 far, Kāne'ohe 0.435 to 0.059 — because a lamp that lights the
ground in front of it puts more of that ground's own colour back into the
frame than one that misses it.
