# The fourth plan: what is sellable, and what has to be true for it

The third plan asked "is each place right?" and answered it. This one asks a
different question, because the third one's question is nearly closed and it
was never the question a customer asks.

**The question here is: if somebody walks into the marine engineering
department at KAUST tomorrow, what opens their mouth?**

Everything below is ordered by that, and everything owed from `r3.md` is
folded into whichever item it actually serves. Work that serves nothing on
this list is not in this plan.

---

## What we are selling, in the order it lands

### 1 · Their reef, with a bibliography

Al Fahal is twenty minutes off the KAUST pier. It is in this platform built
from GEBCO-class bathymetry corrected by ICESat-2 ATL24 photons, habitat from
the Allen Coral Atlas, and an assemblage off Khalil, Bouwmeester and Berumen's
line-intercept transects on nine Thuwal reefs — one of them Al Fahal.

Nobody else can say "your reef" to that room. It is the strongest opening we
have and it is the most finished.

**True today.** Five real places. Al Fahal, Kāne'ohe and Looe Key each carry
habitat maps rather than inferences; cover is measured the same way on all
five and Looe Key's agrees with CREMP's transects to within the classifier
question. Al Fahal delivers 17.6% cover against a survey range of 8–31%.

**The ceiling, and it is the one thing to fix here.** Al Fahal's bathymetry is
**six metres a sample**. Its habitat is measured, its depths are now
calibrated, and the relief *between* samples is interpolation — on the site
sixty-seven of sixty-eight dives are flown in. Nothing else on this list is
limited by anything as coarse.

**Its depths were out by ten metres, and that sentence used to say they were
calibrated.** The restored Al Fahal is Sentinel-2, Stumpf log-ratio,
**uncalibrated**, `surveyed: false`, carrying a standing note that "ICESat-2
ATL24 crosses this reef; two granules would pin the scale". Twelve passes and
**26,726 seafloor photons** later: the seabed was stretched **2.4×** and out by
a median **10.2 m**, spread 1.91 m, rms 10.50 m, against a laser whose own
uncertainty is 0.17 m. The fit is `measured = 0.409 × claimed + 2.96` and takes
rms to **2.00 m**, calibrated between 0.5 and 23.7 m with 1% of the site
extrapolated.

Two things about that. The error was a **bias, not noise** — 1.9 m of spread
under 10.2 m of offset — which is why nothing looked wrong: a reef at thirteen
metres looks like a reef, and this is the reef the pitch opens on. And the sign
**flips with depth**, −10.4 m in the top five metres and +6.6 m between fifteen
and twenty-five, so it was never an offset that a datum would explain. It does
not make the seabed measured. It makes it a derived seabed with a known error
instead of an unknown one.

**Owed.**

  **Looe Key's photogrammetry** — DOI 10.5066/P1QRS3SK. **Down, and verified for
  real this time: 16 of 16 rasters open, none fail** — eight orthomosaics at
  40000 × 40000 RGBA and eight DEMs at 20000 × 20000 float32, 22 GB.

  The sentence that used to stand here — `fetch-release` reporting "all 13
  complete, and every raster among them opens" — was **half untrue, and it was
  quoted here as the reason to trust the fetch.** `opens()` returned True when no
  reader was installed, silently, on the sound reasoning that a fetcher which
  refuses to run without a GIS stack is a fetcher nobody runs. The box has no GDAL.
  So the sizes were checked and **not one raster was ever read.** It now names the
  reader and counts what it opened, or says plainly that it opened none.

  Two more things came out of actually reading them. `tifffile` — pure Python, and
  it handles the BigTIFF these all are — raised on all eight DEMs for want of an LZW
  codec, and the first version of this reported eight perfectly good rasters as
  corrupt, which would have sent somebody re-fetching 9 GB that was already there.
  **A decoder saying it lacks a codec is not a file saying it is broken**, and that
  judgement is made where the verdict is made rather than inside one reader, so
  swapping the reader cannot lose it.

  What is *not* down: the `Orthoaverage` variant (0 of 8), the point clouds (1 of 8)
  and some webview previews. Nothing here asks for those. **The rebuild is
  unblocked.**

  **Rebuilt, and it replays itself.** Looe Key is built: ground from the
  orthomosaic and the habitat polygons, the survey DEM feathered into the
  bathymetry over 11.5% of the square at +0.11 m of datum, 83,055 surveyed
  colonies, 2,440 fish observations over 114 species, water baked from where
  the dive begins. `tools/rebuild` replays the whole chain from the place's own
  record and `coral.usda` and `seabed.f32` come back **byte-identical**. It is
  the first place that can do that.

  It also **was not flyable and nothing said so.** Looe Key was the only one of
  six places with no `seabed.usda` — while its own `site.json` declared
  `layers.terrain: "seabed.usda"`. No terrain USD and no `coral_ground.mdl`, so
  `aim-water` could never have run on it. The restore left a place that reads
  as complete and has no ground to draw.

  **Two provenance leaks, the same shape, both silent.** `tools/ground` merges
  eight named bathymetry surveys and `SQUID_LKR_2022_DEM_1m.tif` and wrote down
  only the *fraction* — under `surveyFraction`, which `make-site` does not read;
  it reads `surveyedFraction`. So a rebuilt Looe Key said `surveyed: true` over
  `surveys: []`: the adjective asserted and not one source named, which is the
  exact thing this item is sold on. And `tools/fauna` and `tools/check-depths`
  write into a place without recording the call, while `tools/rebuild` replays
  a place *from* that record — so a replayed Looe Key came back with its coral,
  its ground, its bathymetry and **no fish**, and nothing about that result
  looks wrong. Both now have tests that walk the real tools and fail against
  the previous ones.

  The pattern in both: the tool that *knew* the answer was not the tool that
  wrote it down, and nothing checked that the two agreed on the key.

  **Al Fahal, rebuilt, and the ten metres nobody could see.** Its seabed is
  Copernicus Sentinel-2, Stumpf log-ratio, **uncalibrated** — `surveyed: false`,
  carrying a standing note that two ATL24 granules would pin its scale. Twelve
  passes and **26,726 seafloor photons** later it was **stretched 2.4×** and out
  by a median **10.2 m**, spread 1.91 m, against a laser whose own uncertainty
  is 0.17 m. Fitted: rms 10.50 m → **2.00 m**.

  And the more useful half: **that 2.00 m is the shallows' number.** 22,430 of
  the 26,726 points sit in the top five metres, so the line is theirs, and what
  is left runs +0.62 m there, −3.24 m by ten and **−16.35 m** between fifteen and
  twenty-five — worse at depth than the seabed it replaced. No shape fixes it.
  Scoring fits on their worst band picked a curve that bought 1.37 m in a
  56-point band and cost 1.34 m in the 22,430-point one, so a longer shape now
  has to earn it. The claims stop growing while the real depth keeps going,
  which is an optical limit seen from underneath, and no monotone rescaling of a
  saturated signal recovers what the sensor never saw.

  So the place records `calibratedToM: 5` — **Al Fahal is calibrated to five
  metres and below that it is not** — and that is the number somebody planning a
  dive needs. Deeper truth needs an instrument that went there; ICESat-2 gave
  140 points below ten metres over this reef and that is all it has.

  **Also rebuilt:** Kāne'ohe with the `hawaii` mix — which had been in
  `tools/zonation` in detail for a fortnight while `--assemblage`'s help still
  read "caribbean or red-sea", so I rebuilt a Pacific reef with no mix named at
  all. There is no `choices` on that flag; the help text is the only index of
  those mixes there is, and a test now requires it to name every key in the
  dict. And `red-sea`, which is a *constructed* fringing reef and now says so
  where its cover used to say nothing.

  **Al Fahal's water, finally, is its own.** Shushah carried Jerlov IB from
  Overmans & Agusti 2020's Kd(PAR) at the pelagic station **offshore of KAUST**
  — a reading taken twenty minutes from Al Fahal and carried 70 km to a
  different reef, while Al Fahal itself rendered through an assumed 1C. It now
  carries that citation, which is the place the measurement is actually about.

  **Every cover figure lands inside its cited range.** `tools/deliver` runs on
  all five reef places and each one's own record agrees with the raster it just
  wrote, to the threshold rather than the file:

  | place | cover delivered | against |
  |---|---|---|
  | Looe Key | 3.94% | CREMP transects, ~4% stony |
  | Al Fahal | 19.11% | 8–31% over nine Thuwal reefs |
  | Shushah | 22.92% | Allen Coral Atlas, this reef |
  | Kāne'ohe | 39.89% | 37.9–88.0% across the bay, 2014 baseline |
  | red-sea | 43.55% | chosen; a constructed reef |

  **And item 2's pages are back past where they were.** `tools/provenance --all`
  now reads Looe Key **12 measured**, Al Fahal **4**, Kāne'ohe 4, Shushah 4,
  against the 2 and 0 the restored copies gave. The table in item 2 claims 13
  and 3.

  **What is still owed here.** The places are rebuilt *on disk*; their published
  packages are still the older builds, so a dive today would fly the 60-metre Al
  Fahal. Publishing is one command per place and it changes what every dive
  flies, so it is left to be run deliberately rather than as the tail of a
  rebuild. `red-sea` cannot have its medium aimed at all — `aim-water` needs the
  ground material and a constructed place has no reference to build one from —
  and `thuwal-deep` is 638 m of GMRT with no reef, which is what it should be.

  **It was already fetched.** 9.9 GB of the release is on the box: all eight
  orthomosaics at 40000 × 40000 and 5 mm a pixel. What is *not* there is the
  elevation: `dem-urls.txt` lists eight and the fetch log records three, so of
  the eight 1 cm DEMs **two open** (C-1, D-1), three are truncated downloads
  that are the right size in a listing and raise on the first read, and three
  were never fetched. The colony detections for all eight tiles survive, so
  this happened after they were made.

  **`tools/close-up` cuts a patch at the resolution it was surveyed at.** Ten
  metres square, 1,048,576 cells at 0.98 cm, coloured at 2.44 mm — and no
  grown reef, because at a centimetre *the coral is the ground*. A brain coral
  is a metre of surveyed surface with the grooves in it. A sea fan is not
  there at all, because two cameras cannot both see through one, and saying so
  beats putting one back.

  **And no tiled stock surface either.** `make-site` now drops it on any place
  sampled finer than ten centimetres: the stock photographs exist to stand in
  for what is missing between samples, and here nothing is missing. The first
  square metre rendered from this patch came out as Normandy beach pebbles
  with the reef nowhere in it.

  **The correction I had to unwrite.** The first version divided the
  orthomosaic by `exp(-2 * depth / length)` per pixel to take the water out of
  it. It came back magenta, clipped white, red lifted twelve and a half times.
  Two things were wrong: SQUID-5 is a camera a metre or two off the bottom, so
  the light does not cross the column twice — and the published product
  measures **R/G 1.009, B/G 1.021**, neutral to one per cent, where a raw
  photograph at five metres would be R/G 0.16. Somebody had already taken the
  water out. The tool measures and reports that now instead of correcting it.

  **A nadir square-metre view**, at 1.15 m, which is where a 24 mm lens sees a
  metre across. It exposed a real bug: looking straight down with world up as
  the camera's up is degenerate — the roll comes out as whatever the
  arithmetic produces — so a near-nadir view takes **north** as up now, which
  is what an orthomosaic uses, and the two are comparable without anybody
  working out a rotation. `looking-down` had been living with it.

  **The acceptance is met, and finding out cost more than making it.** A
  square metre of render sits beside the USGS photograph of that square metre
  — aimed at a 0.96 m head the survey detected at patch-local (−0.02, +0.40) —
  and the same dark diagonal streak, the same bright patch lower-left, the
  same dark band along the bottom edge and the same rounded rim appear in
  both. The difference is the water and the light, not the reef.

  **The bug underneath it: there are three `place()` methods.** `Tour`,
  `Ladder` and `Stills`, and `Stills` is the one that renders a sheet. Two
  fixes went into the first two by string replacement and neither changed
  anything, which read as "the fix did nothing" — the rule at the bottom of
  this plan, in the one shape it had not taken yet. `Tour.place` had solved
  the degenerate nadir basis months ago with a comment explaining it;
  `Stills.place` never got it, so **`looking-down` has been rendering at a
  roll nobody chose for as long as it has existed**.

  **And frames say where they are now.** A nadir frame *is* an orthophoto, and
  one that does not carry where it is, how wide it is and which way is north
  is a picture rather than a measurement. `stills_where` records the eye, the
  target, the right and up vectors read back out of the camera matrix, the
  width in metres and whether it is nadir. With those, a pixel is a place and
  the comparison needs no search.

  **What is not a valid test, learned three times.** Greyscale correlation
  between a render and a flat-lit orthomosaic. At the aimed position it reads
  **−0.197** while the eye sees the same features unmistakably, because the
  render shades slopes the photograph lights evenly and matching structure
  anti-correlates. It sent me hunting a phantom misalignment across eight
  orientations and a grid of offsets. The alignment is confirmed instead by an
  independent measurement — the survey put a 0.96 m head at that point and a
  head fills the frame — which is what should have been used from the start.

  **The DEMs, and why they were missing.** `tools/reference` listed the
  release with every file's exact byte count and then generated a shell script
  to fetch them:

      [ -s NAME ] || curl -sL --retry 3 -o NAME URL

  `-s` is true of any file that is not empty, so a download interrupted part
  way left a file the script would never touch again — no resume, no check
  against the size it already knew, and a cheerful exit either way. That is
  how 4.53 GB of the highest-resolution asset on this platform went quietly
  absent for months.

  `tools/fetch-release` replaces it: reads the manifest, resumes what is part
  way with `curl -C -`, moves aside anything *longer* than it should be
  because that is not a short copy of the right file, comes back four times,
  then says what is actually there and **exits non-zero if anything is still
  wrong**. It opens every raster it fetched as well, because a size check
  catches truncation and not corruption. The DEMs are coming down now.

  **And the square-metre view is taken only where the coral is the ground.**
  At 1.15 m above the *floor*, on a place whose colonies are grown onto the
  seabed rather than surveyed as part of it, the camera ends up inside one —
  Al Fahal's came back a solid sheet of yellow, which is a correct picture of
  the inside of a Porites and no use to anybody. The test is the same one
  `make-site` uses to decide a place wants no tiled stock surface: finer than
  ten centimetres a sample. One rule, two places, same reason.
  **Better bathymetry for Al Fahal**, or the honest admission that six metres
  is what exists and the site is drawn at that.
  **Thuwal Deep's habitat** is still inferred from the shape of its own
  seabed. It is the one place left without a map, and the Atlas does not go to
  550 m.

### 2 · Every number says whether anybody measured it

This is the quiet one that makes a research customer sit up, because it is
what makes output *publishable*. A cover figure that traces to a transect is
citable; one that came out of a pipeline is not, and most simulators cannot
tell you which they gave you.

**True today.** `provenance.json` ships with every deliverable and marks each
column measured or not, per place — at Looe Key the positions and diameters
are observations and the growth form is not. Places record where their cover
target came from, whether the budget could pay for it, and which of their
seabed was built past a sensor's limit. `tools/cite` records a paper as a
reading rather than a fetch.

**The page exists.** `tools/provenance` writes one per place from the records
the place already keeps: every claim it makes, tagged **measured**, **derived**
or **chosen**, with where each came from. Self-contained — no fonts, no
scripts, nothing to fetch — so it survives being emailed, printed and put in a
slide.

Derived and chosen are kept apart on purpose, because they are not the same:
derived is arithmetic on something real, chosen is a person deciding. A page
that collapsed them would be doing exactly what it exists to stop.

The summary line is the pitch, and it is not flattering everywhere:

| | measured | derived | chosen |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **looe-key** | **13** | 3 | 0 |
| looe-key-e157n7 (the 1 cm patch) | 3 | 0 | 1 |
| al-fahal | 3 | 14 | 3 |
| kaneohe | 3 | 12 | 2 |
| red-sea | 2 | 13 | 2 |
| thuwal-deep | 2 | 12 | 2 |

Looe Key is the place to lead with and this says why in one line. It also says
what the others are, which is the point: a customer who is told "13 measured,
0 chosen" about one reef and "3 measured, 3 chosen" about another knows
exactly what they are buying.

Two faults it caught on its first render, both in the record rather than the
page. A row came out tagged **measured** with a value of "inferred from slope
and relief" and a source saying "habitat polygons from a survey" — three
fields disagreeing in one row, because the tag and the value were computed
from different tests. And Looe Key's cover claimed to be over "the reef
habitat this place was planted into", of a reef nobody planted: `cover_over`
wrote that sentence for every caller, and the caller is the only one that
knows. It says its own now.

**One page for the platform.** `tools/provenance --all` now also writes an
index: the table above, generated, sorted by what was measured, each row a
place with its one-line description and a bar of the three colours. That is
the slide; the place pages are what it links to.

**The same discipline on a dive.** `tools/provenance --dive <log>` is the
place page's twin for a run: every condition the dive was flown in, and who
said so. It needed a fourth word. A place is authored — every field of it was
filled in by somebody, so nothing there is unclaimed — but a dive is flown
against defaults, and a field nobody touched still gets a number: still
water, ordinary seawater, a flat surface. That is not *chosen*, because
nobody chose it, so it is **assumed**, in grey, and the page tells the reader
to look at the grey rows first.

The gap this closed was real and had been open from the start. The control
plane has always known that conditions are either observed at an instant or
constructed by a person, and it refuses the ones whose instant disagrees with
their kind — a constraint written, enforced and tested. The runtime read the
numbers out of them and threw the claim away. So a dive record said
1025 kg/m³ whether a CTD had measured it or the default had stood in, and
still water whether the sea was calm or the box was empty. Those are not the
same dive and the record now says so: `conditions_from`, said once at the top
of every log, carrying both halves — the numbers, and where each came from.

Two things it makes visible that nothing else did. Nothing in the water
measures a current — not a satellite, not a wave buoy — so an *observed*
condition set still has a chosen current, and the page says so rather than
letting the word "observed" cover the whole row. And density has three
different provenances depending on how it arrived: measured when somebody
read it, **derived** when it came out of a CTD's salinity and temperature
through the equation of state, assumed when it is the constant.

### 3 · What comes out is a science product

A score and a video is not a deliverable. A colony inventory a reef scientist
opens in a spreadsheet, points they drop on a map, and a coverage raster they
put under their own layers — that is.

**True today.** `tools/deliver` writes `colonies.csv`, `colonies.geojson` in
WGS84, `cover.asc` + `.prj` as an ESRI grid that needs no GDAL, cover by
growth form, and the provenance above. The georeference is the exact inverse
of the expression each place was sampled with, written out in words so it can
be checked without reading the code.

**Deliverables from a flown mission.** `tools/deliver --dive <recording>`
writes what one piece of work produced: `track.csv` and `track.geojson`,
`planting.csv` and `.geojson` for an outplant, `coverage.geojson` for a
survey, the conditions page from item 2, and `provenance.json`.

The vocabulary is not the place's, and that is the point. Nothing in a
simulated mission is *measured*, so the columns are **truth**, **believed**
and **planned**: the simulator knows where the vehicle was, the vehicle only
knows what its navigation told it, the plan knows where it was supposed to
be. Every row of `track.csv` carries the first two and the distance between
them. **No real vehicle can produce that column**, and it is the single
strongest thing on the table — a restoration that plants on dead reckoning
plants a reef nobody can find again, and this is where that shows up before
anybody has spent a boat day.

A prerequisite had to be fixed first, and it had been broken from the start:
a recording carried the seabed as a grid of heights and **nothing that said
which reef it was or where on the Earth it sat**. Everything a mission
produced was in metres from a centre nobody had written down. The manifest
now carries the place, its centre, and what it was sampled at, and
`deliver --dive` refuses a recording that cannot say — rather than writing a
track that looks georeferenced and is not.

The survey product is written as cells and not as an ESRI grid on purpose: a
survey rectangle is stated along the vehicle's own heading, so it is square
to the mission and not to north, and an axis-aligned raster of it would be a
resampling presented as a measurement.

**And the pack on top of all of it.** By the end there are a lot of pages —
what anybody measured about the reef, what each dive produced, what the day
turns on, what the whole grid would take, whether round two holds against
round one. Each is right on its own; together they are a directory listing.
`tools/pack` writes the page that goes on top: every kind, one line each
read from the JSON beside it, and a link — and **every kind that is not
there, in red, with the command that would make it**. A pack that quietly
omitted the missing pieces would read as complete.

`pack --for <place> --dives <recordings>` builds the whole thing, in the
order the pieces depend on each other. That chain was worked out by hand
the first time and then written down, because a chain that lives in
somebody's shell history is run differently the second time.

Assembling a real one out of real output found three things a fixture
never would: the pack counted a *mission's* provenance.json as a place's
and called the dive report "the reef" twice; the mission report's map had
scaled itself to the ten-centimetre scribble a station-keeper made and
hidden the 250 m it was asked to travel; and delivering every recording on
the box refused twelve hundred of them with twelve hundred copies of one
sentence. All three are fixed, and none of them would have shown up in a
test.

**A report somebody can hand to a funder.** `report.html`, in the same folder
and in the same plain style as the provenance pages: the headline numbers,
the track drawn as an inline SVG with both lines on it, the water it was
flown in claim by claim, and what every other file in the folder is. Self
contained — an SVG drawn from the numbers rather than a render, no scripts,
nothing to fetch — so it survives being emailed and printed.

### 4 · Controllers are the product, and this is the least finished

Named first in every conversation about what this platform is for, and the
furthest behind. It is the difference between selling a simulator and selling
a place where somebody's autonomy gets better.

**What is actually true today, stated plainly because it has been overstated.**
There are four controllers — `pursue`, `wary`, `ponder`, `asking` — and all
four are **built in**: Python classes inside the runtime, hard-coded into the
helm's registry. They are baselines, which is a legitimate thing to be, and
they are not what a customer writes.

A customer's controller comes in a different door entirely: `packages/
sdk-python`, containerised, deployed, talking ROS 2 across a process boundary,
appearing inside the runtime as **one** controller called `stack`.

**Three things follow, and they are this plan's first real work.**

  **The benchmark cannot judge a customer's controller.** It selects by name
  out of the helm's dict, which only ever holds built-ins plus one entry
  called `stack` — so there is no way to bench somebody's controller, and no
  way to bench two of them against each other, because both are `stack`. A
  bench that can only rank our own baselines is ranking the wrong thing.
  **Two copies of the interface.** `Command` and `Observation` exist in the
  SDK and in the runtime as separate dataclasses. Their fields are identical
  today. Nothing makes them identical tomorrow, and the SDK's own docstring
  promises "the interface is the runtime's own". This is the same fault as the
  two cover functions and the two hardness functions, in the place where it
  would be a customer who found it.
  **Nothing has ever been deployed through the SDK and benched.** The whole
  path — write, containerise, deploy, fly, score — has not been walked end to
  end by anything.

**One interface, in one file.** Done. `Observation`, `Command` and
`Parameter` are declared once, in `coral_city/interface.py`, and both the SDK
and the runtime import that file — `base.Observation is coral_city.Observation`
is a test, not a hope. The interface lives in the published package rather
than inside the runtime because the published package is the promise; the
image copies it in, and the stamp `tools/look` checks now covers both trees,
because an image built from half a hash swears it is current when it is not.

They had already drifted, which is the point. The runtime's observation
carried the sonar's nearest return and the whole fan; the SDK's did not — so
somebody writing against the published interface could not see **the one
thing a controller learns that nobody told it**, which is that there is
something in the water. Meanwhile the SDK's observation said whether the
position was an estimate and the runtime's did not, and nothing on the
runtime's side filled that field in at all. Both are now on one class and
the runtime says which it is handing over.

**A controller flies under its own name.** Half done, and the half that was
blocking everything. Everything arriving over ROS 2 arrived as `stack` — the
*slot*, not the controller — so a run record said a dive was flown by "stack"
and two of somebody's controllers on one bench were both called that.
The dive now carries what the platform deployed (`autonomySlug`,
`autonomyName`, out of `dive.autonomy_stack`, through the agent's brief), the
stack controller flies under it, and `who_flew` — which is what the bench
reads to attribute a result — reports names instead of slots. A stack nobody
deployed falls back to the ROS node it named itself, and only then to
"stack".
`tools/bench run --controller <slug>` now accepts a deployed controller:
anything not built in is looked up among the organisation's own and attached
to each dive, and a name that is neither is **refused** with the list of what
there is — a bench row attributed to a controller that never flew is worse
than a bench that would not run.

**Walking it end to end is blocked, and finding out why was worth more than
the walk.** `station-hold` is deployed and registered; `bench run
--controller station-hold` defines the dive, the agent claims it, pulls the
place, starts the simulator — and then the simulator stops. Every event up to
`water_is` arrives and nothing after it; Kit says "app ready"; the main
thread is in native code with eleven cores busy and nothing is written to the
work directory again. Two runs, two different places, identical stop.

What that did to the dive is the part worth keeping. The agent waits five
minutes for the simulator to say `bridge_open` before it starts the
controller, because nobody starts the controller before the vehicle is
powered. It never came. The agent logged *"the vehicle never came up; the
dive continues untended"* — and **carried on**: twenty minutes of card time,
the tasks scoring an empty vehicle, and a result that reads as a controller
achieving nothing. That is indistinguishable from a controller that flew
badly, and it is the one thing a benchmark must never confuse. It now fails
the run and says which half went wrong.

The five-minute wait was wrong in shape as well as length: a simulator
opening a reef of seven hundred thousand colonies takes longer and *says so
the whole way*. It is now five minutes of silence rather than five minutes in
total, with a twenty-minute cap — a deadline that ignores evidence of
progress gave up on scenes that were loading perfectly well.

**What the stop is and is not.** The simulator reaches `water_is`, Kit says
"app ready", and then nothing: no further event, nothing written to the work
directory again, eleven cores busy, and the main thread inside native code
with no Python frame on it (`py-spy` against the container's pid). It is not
slow — it is stopped, and it stays stopped for as long as it is left.

Ruled out, each by running the same image by hand:

| suspected | tested by | verdict |
| --- | --- | --- |
| the place | `tools/look looe-key` renders five frames | not it |
| the image | same digest, by hand, completes | not it |
| the brief (a task, autonomy, batch) | the failed run's own `dive.json`, by hand | not it |
| the card | card 0 by hand completes; card 1 completes | not it |
| GPU memory pressure | another process holds 26 GB of card 0 throughout, both ways | not it |
| the internal network | `--network` with `Internal: true`, by hand | not it |
| `PidsLimit: 512`, `CapDrop: ALL`, `no-new-privileges`, the 512 MB `noexec` `/tmp` | all four together, by hand | not it |
| our own output buffering | `say` already flushes; the container's whole log is frozen | not it |
| **the working directory** | **`-w /work` by hand, all else identical** | **it** |

What was left, from a full `docker inspect` diff of a hung container against
a working one, was **one field**, and it was the answer: the agent gave
every container `WorkingDir: /work`. That is the agent's own directory. It
does not exist in the simulator's image, so Docker creates it owned by root,
and the image runs as user 1234 — and Kit resolves its caches and its logs
against the working directory. Two runs of the same image, same card, same
network, same limits, differing only in that flag: with `/isaac-sim` it
reaches `bridge_open` in two minutes; with `/work` it stops after `water_is`
and burns eleven cores until it is killed.

Fixed: `WorkingDir` is set only when the spec actually stages work there.

Two things this cost, both worth remembering. It was **silent** — no error,
no exit, nothing on the output, just a process using a lot of CPU. And what
the platform did about it made it worse: the agent waited five minutes for
the vehicle, gave up, logged "the dive continues untended", and flew the dive
anyway, so the failure arrived as a *controller that achieved nothing*.

**The Autonomy screen says what each controller flew**, not only that it was

A comparison is a comparison when the dive is the same dive, and the bench
already guarantees that and says so in the name it gives each row — `bench ·

whether there was anything in the water all match and only the controller

a tie rather than as a win, and a controller with nothing in common is left out
rather than reported as a nil-all draw. Nothing outside the bench is compared: a
dive somebody set up by hand has no counterpart and is not pretended to have one.

Adding "through things" to that name mattered more than it looks — without it a
dive flown past three nursery frames and a dive flown through open water read as
one trial, and the difference between them would have been reported as skill.

**What a bench costs, which bounds all of this.** A rendering dive advances
about **ten seconds of simulated time per sixty of wall clock** — measured
three ways on 27 September, including start-to-finish on a 1500 s survey
that took 2 h 25 m. So one task is a couple of hours and the eight-task
`standard` suite is **a day per controller**, which is why everything above
was walked with `--tasks` subsets.

**Found, and it was not the hardware.** Four runs of the same brief on the
same card, each ruling out a suspect by measurement rather than by argument:

| how the dive is run | rate | picture |
| --- | --- | --- |
| the shell extension, which is what the agent launches | **0.17×** | yes |
| the image's own batch runner | 3.3× | no |
| the batch runner, rendering only when the recording wants a frame | 5.2× | no |
| the batch runner with no renderer started at all, on no GPU | **15×** | no |

That last row is the best case and not the general one. **What the vehicle
carries costs more than the place does**, measured on the same hull, same task,
same reef, and consistent across two different tasks:

| | flat floor | real reef |
| --- | --- | --- |
| no sensors fitted | 15.8× | 14.0× |
| **the vehicle's sensors fitted** | 5.1× | **4.2×** |

The imaging sonar costs about 3.1× and a REMUS's multibeam another 2× (it runs at
2.0×), while the real reef's own heightfield costs 12%. A real bench dive fits sensors, so 4× is the number to plan with:
a `standard` suite of eight tasks is **under an hour** per controller rather than
a day. The 15× row was a dive whose published vehicle package listed no sensors,
which is worth knowing separately.

Contention was not it — the same rate with two dives sharing the box and with
one alone. The hardware was not it — one dive alone leaves the GPU near idle
and uses 139% CPU of 128 cores. The video was not it either: two identical
dives side by side, one writing video and one with the recorder unavailable,
advanced 5.005 simulated seconds per 30 of wall, both of them, to three
decimal places.

It was the renderer, and the renderer was never needed. A step costs 0.256
milliseconds and is pure numpy — not one of the fourteen things a step does
asks the stage a question — and both the multibeam and the imaging sonar march
their rays against the place's heightfield rather than against its triangles.
What the stage was being asked for was how big the place is, and the place
already says, in the same description its heightfield comes from. **The dry
dive's poses file has the same md5 as the drawn dive's.** Same trajectory, to
the byte, with no Kit, no stage and no card.

**And it is not only the bench.** `tools/again` compares two survey rounds off
their coverage and their mission records; `tools/deliver` builds the report, the
track, the coverage and the bathymetry off the poses and the sensors. None of
them opens a frame. So a comparability round — the thing NCW's second survey
actually needs — is a dive nobody looks at too: the 1500-second survey running
while this was written took **two and a half hours** drawn, and the same dive
undrawn is about six minutes. That is the difference between a round two somebody
schedules and a round two somebody talks about.

**What it gives up is exactly one thing, and it is not nothing.** `deliver`'s
`stills_from` reads a frame or two out of `dive.mp4` for the report and returns
nothing when there is no video — so an undrawn round still produces every number
and every geometry, and produces a report with no photographs in it. That file's
own words for why that matters: "a page about a dive with nothing of the dive on
it is a spreadsheet with a headline." So this is a choice per round and not a
default: a comparability round that exists to be read by `again` can be undrawn,
and a package going to a funder probably should not be. It is the caller's call,
which is why it is not wired to `pictures: false` here.

  the objective carried `pictures: false`;
  the brief the agent wrote said `"drawn": false`;
  the simulator container ran `/isaac-sim/python.sh` — the batch runner — and
  **not** `kit/kit coral_city.kit`;
  the dive reached 880 of its 1200 simulated seconds in about two and a half
  minutes, roughly **6× real time** against 0.17× drawn;
  the record holds `poses.jsonl`, `sensors.jsonl`, `task.jsonl` and
  `manifest.json`, and **no `dive.mp4`**;
  and the row landed in the record and read back through `bench show` —
  `pursue` at 17.4% on the reach over Looe Key, 2.18 m of drift, 17.09 Wh.

A 1200-second task that took two hours takes three and a half minutes, through
the platform, on a queue, with nothing hand-run.

So the worker now asks what a dive is *for*. A dive somebody looks at is drawn;
a dive somebody measures is not, and `tools/bench` is the caller that says so
(`pictures: false` on the objective — silence still means pictures, because a
dive set up by hand is a dive somebody wants to see). The `standard` suite goes
from a day per controller to something under a quarter of an hour, and it no
longer needs a GPU at all — which means benching is not rationed by the one box.

What is still true is that a dive flown by a **controller in a container**
cannot outrun the wall clock, because the controller publishes on it: those
pace to real time as they always did. The saving is real for every dive flown
by something in-process, and for the rest the renderer is simply no longer
charged on top.

| `ponder` | 48.8% | 5.71 m | 8.88 Wh | 165 |

All four rows stand. `ponder` scores twice what `pursue` does on two thirds of the

which is a trade somebody choosing a controller would want to know and could not

(`thinkS=1.5`, which is what that parameter is for: "the platform must fly
correctly while one is outstanding, and that is not something to find out for the
first time with a model on the other end") halves the number of thoughts and costs
a tenth of a point.

**Getting to "all four rows stand" took three wrong turns, and they are worth more
than the rows.** The question was whether a dive running faster than the clock
charges a thinking controller honestly, and the answer was in the code the whole
time:

> `Thinking._deliver` holds a thought until `simulated >= asked_at + took`, where
> `took` is the **real** seconds the thread spent. "A thought that took a second
> and a half in reality costs a second and a half of the dive, whether the dive is
> running at real time on a graphics card or a thousand times faster in a test —
> otherwise the same controller on a quicker computer would appear to think for
> free, and every benchmark would be measuring the machine."

Real seconds are charged as simulated seconds, one for one, at any rate. Nothing
needed adding. What I did instead:

  **Added a guard** to both runners and the dry bench pacing a dive to real time
  whenever a controller could think, on the strength of the interactive shell's
  comment rather than a reading of `_deliver`.
  **Argued** that it could not matter for a default `ponder` because `thinkS`
  defaults to zero — and separately that a thought arrives `thinkS × rate`
  simulated seconds late, which is exactly what `_deliver` prevents.
  **Measured**, finally, and the guard had moved `ponder`'s quick suite from
  **48.8% to 23.3%** — a twenty-five point change to a result that was already
  right, landing it on top of `pursue` and looking for all the world like a
  finding.

Two measurements closed it. The same brief flown **three times** gave `50%,
2.42 m, 12.51 Wh` — three times, to every printed digit — so `ponder` is
perfectly reproducible and the determinism this platform promises holds. And
`_deliver`, read rather than assumed, says the charge was never missing.

The guard is removed from all three places, with the reason written where somebody
would otherwise put it back and a test in each holding it out. **What it cost was
an hour of box time and three wrong paragraphs here; what keeping it would have
cost is every `ponder` row on the bench being quietly wrong.** The lesson is the
one already written at the top of this section and evidently not learned twice
over: *measure it rather than reason about it.* Both wrong turns today were
arguments that felt complete — "nothing a step does touches the stage" was one of
those too, and that one happened to be right, which is the trap.

**And one number in those rows was a lie until today.** `bluerov2-heavy` and
`remus-100` state a hotel load and no capacity in their packages, so every bench
row for either read **0.00 Wh** — which does not say "unknown", it says this
controller used no energy, in the column somebody choosing between two
controllers looks at first. Found by flying them. The Heavy now carries the
standard BlueROV2 pack, because it is the same battery in the same enclosure —
the Heavy Retrofit changes the thrusters and the buoyancy — and that is recorded
as derived rather than measured. The REMUS still states none, because this
repository has no source for its published energy, and the column now reads `—`
rather than a number nobody can stand behind.

**What the bench could not do, and why that reading was wrong.** `bench dry`
flew in-process over a floor pinned flat at twelve metres with nothing in the
water, so `wary` — `pursue` with the imaging sonar on — came out *identical to
`pursue` to the last decimal*. The obvious conclusion was that comparing
controllers that differ in **seeing** needs the simulator. It does not, and the
conclusion was wrong twice over:

  *The bottom does not need a renderer.* Both the multibeam and the imaging
  sonar march their rays against the place's **heightfield**, so
  `bench dry --over <place>` flies a real reef with no graphics at all, at 14×
  real time against 15× over a flat floor — an eleven percent cost for a real
  bottom.
  *Neither do the things in the water.* What a vehicle can run into comes from
  the brief's **layout** — `World(brief["layout"])` — and not from the stage.
  The bench had nothing to run into because its brief carried no layout, which
  is a line of code and not a limit of the approach. A place package does not
  ship one either: a survey holds a seabed and its coral, and everything
  somebody *arranged* is a separate thing the platform keeps. So a dry bench
  makes its own, off the task's own geometry, which is the only way to be sure
  the things are in the way rather than somewhere the vehicle was never going.

`bench dry --through` does that, and the row says so — a dive flown through
things is not comparable with one flown through open water, and every row
already in the record was flown through open water.

**And `bench run` does it too, which I first wrote down as impossible.** I said
there was no endpoint that creates a layout. There is — `POST
/api/v1/cities/{cityId}/layouts` starts one, `POST
/api/v1/layouts/{layoutId}/versions` saves it, and a dive already takes a
`layoutVersionId` with no publishing step. What remained was the reference track,
and the record turns out to carry everything needed for it:

  `GET /api/v1/dives/{diveId}/runs/{runId}/artefacts` hands back the recording's
  files with URLs that resolve from a laptop over Tailscale — 1.9 MB of
  `poses.jsonl` fetched in one call.
  **A pose states the position and the altitude**, so the bottom under a point on
  the path is `z - altitudeM` — measured by the dive rather than read out of the
  place's heightfield, which is the thing a laptop has no copy of. The one part of
  this that needed the place turns out not to.
  The reference is `pursue` flying the same task through open water, which is a
  bench row in its own right — so it is looked up by its own name, flown only when
  it is not already there, and every controller compared on that task meets the
  same three frames.

Verified: a `wary · transect · through things` dive was defined with layout
version `ver_01M3H22JY1WETJJP0E5R05SAZ5`, and the brief the runtime received
carried three nursery frames, two metres across and 3.6 m tall, standing on
bottoms at 7.88, 8.13 and 7.34 m — each of those depths taken from the reference
dive's own poses. The arrangement is a layout of the place, so it is visible,
re-flyable and attributable rather than a private detail of whichever process
happened to fly the dive.

**Both of the mistakes it took to get there were the same mistake**, and both
were found by measuring rather than by reading:

  A nursery frame is 1.2 m tall and a transect holds 3 m of altitude. Three
  frames dead on the line are three frames the vehicle flies a metre and a half
  over. The thing is now chosen by the altitude the task holds, and a task flown
  too high to meet anything in the palette — a survey at five metres, a search
  at eight — is **refused**, because a row claiming things it passed over would
  make a bench that rewards flying high.
  A thing that lands on the ground and does not say where the ground is sits at
  **zero**. Three posts three metres tall therefore stood from the surface down,
  in the air fifteen metres above the vehicle. Each thing now carries the depth of
  the bottom under it, out of the same heightfield the dive flies over.
  A task's geometry is relative to where the dive starts and a layout is in the
  world's own coordinates, so frames placed off the origin sat behind the vehicle.
  And the vehicle does not fly the line the task states: over a 200 m transect in
  0.13 m/s of current it bows about **twelve metres** south of it, so frames on
  that line were passed with five metres to spare — measured by printing the track
  against the arrangement. They go on a reference track now, flown once by
  `pursue` with nothing in the way, which answers all four at once: it says where
  the vehicle goes, at what altitude, over which bottom, in the world's own
  coordinates. With that, the frames land 0.6–1.1 m off the track and the dive
  strikes them.

The arrangement findings survive — they were verified against printed positions

**Where item 4 stands, and what is left.** What was true this morning: four
built-in controllers, a bench that cost a day per controller, and an Autonomy page
that listed uploads. What is true now:

  A bench dive is **undrawn**, so a suite costs minutes and no GPU — verified end
  to end through the platform, on a queue, from a laptop.
  The rows **mean something**: `pursue` and `wary` identical in open water and
  apart with things in it, and both driving into three frames of three, which is a
  finding about a controller rather than about the bench.
  An arrangement can be put in the water by **either** bench, placed off a
  reference flight, saved as a layout of the place so it is re-flyable by anybody.
  The page **compares** two controllers only where the dive was the same dive, and

  A thought costs what it costs, in all three runners.

**And then the thing that had been quietly wrong all along.** Going to write the
SDK example that avoids obstacles — the customer-side counterpart of `wary`, since
the bench can now put frames in front of one — I found there was no such example,
and then why there was none: **a deployed controller could not see the sonar.**
The runtime publishes `/sonar/scan` and has all along, described in its own bridge
as "the message a collision avoider expects"; each vehicle's catalogue declares the
topic; `Observation.sonar` is documented in the interface down to the trap of
steering on the nearest return instead of the gap. And `coral_city/ros.py`
subscribed to three topics — depth, attitude, Doppler — and not that one. So
`Observation.sonar` and `Observation.seen` were **always None** for anything
deployed through the SDK, and the one instrument a controller learns anything from
that nobody told it was unreadable through the public interface.

It subscribes now, converts `LaserScan`'s infinity to the NaN the interface
specifies, keeps the last fan between sweeps (a fan arrives a few times a second
and a controller is asked twenty times a second), and the tank passes the fan
through both its observation paths so the thing can be tried before it is
deployed.

**Verified live, on the wire, during a dive**: `avoid` deployed as
`stack_01M3H6R8Z65ZNGR7QERSV2KGA7` and pinned by digest, flown on the platform
through three frames, and inside its own container —

    ros2 topic info /sonar/scan
    Type: sensor_msgs/msg/LaserScan
    Publisher count: 1
    Subscription count: 1

That subscription count was **zero** for every controller anybody deployed before
today. `examples/avoid.py` is the example: it flies where it is sent, looks
across the fan for the widest run of empty beams, steers at the middle of it,
eases off harder the closer the thing is, and commits to a side until the way is
clear.

**Two more of the same kind, found by reading the publishers against the
subscribers, and both now fixed:**

  **Nothing published the bottom.** A Doppler log measures the seabed to get
  velocity over the ground, so it knows the range to it and every real one reports
  it; this bridge published the twist and not the range, so `Observation.floor` was
  always None for a deployed controller. Reef work is altitude work and a transect
  is *scored* on holding altitude, so a customer's controller could not fly the task
  it was being measured on. There is a `/dvl/range` now — `sensor_msgs/Range`, with
  the log's own bounds, and **infinity when the bottom is out of them**, because
  losing bottom lock is a thing that happens and a controller holding an altitude
  has to handle it. The SDK turns that into `floor` and says **unknown** rather than
  stale when the lock goes, since a controller told a stale floor holds an altitude
  over where the bottom used to be and flies into the next head.
  **Nothing published the objective.** There is a `/task` now, **latched** — the
  autonomy container starts around the same time as the dive, and a goal published
  once to nobody is a goal nobody has — and republished whenever it changes, because
  a mission's stages are separate goals and a controller told the first and nothing
  after would fly the whole mission as its opening leg. It is a `String` of JSON
  rather than a message type: an objective is a small open document whose fields
  differ by task, and inventing a message per task would make adding one a change to
  the wire. `Controller.tasked(goal)` is the hook, the same signature and default as
  the runtime's own, and the tank delivers it in the same order the platform does.

`avoid.py` reads both now — the altitude off the task, the bottom off the log — so
it can be put on the same path as the baseline it is compared against. That was the
thing that made the comparison impossible rather than merely unfair.

Together those two are the sharpest thing found today: **the baselines a
customer's controller is benched against are handed the task and the bottom, and
the customer's controller is handed neither.** Every comparison on the Autonomy
page is fair between two built-ins or between two deployed stacks, and tilted
between the two kinds. That is worth fixing before anybody is invited to write one,
and it is a topic and a hook rather than a rethink.

**And it is worse than unfair — it makes the comparison impossible.** `avoid` was
flown on the platform through the same three frames `pursue` and `wary` met, and
the row reads `struck 0` against their 3 of 3, which looks like the SDK controller
being the only one that avoided anything. It is not. Measured against the
arrangement, its closest approach to the three frames was **26.4, 22.8 and 19.2
metres** — it never came near them. Not having the bottom, it holds a depth rather
than an altitude, so it flies a different path from the altitude-holding reference
the frames were placed on, and a controller that cannot be put on the same path
cannot be compared on the same obstacles. The `struck` column would have read as a
triumph.

**A third gap of the same family, and this one was the worst.** The tank — "a tank
on your own machine", the thing a customer tries a controller in before deploying
it — built its dive with **no `vehiclePath`**, so `switch_on_the_sonar` and every
sibling found no package and fitted **nothing**. No sonar, no CTD, no modem, and no
**tether**. A BlueROV2 without its tether is an easier vehicle than the one anybody
owns, and a platform dive always fits one. Measured on the same brief, the SDK's own
station-hold example in a third of a knot of current:

| the tank | score | thruster effort |
| --- | --- | --- |
| as it was — no package, no tether | **1.000** | 0.019 |
| fitting what the vehicle carries | **0.276** | 0.024 |

So the SDK's central promise — the same class in the tank, on the platform, and on
a vehicle; "a controller that holds here holds there" — was false in the direction
that flatters. A hold tuned in the tank drifts when deployed.

**And the 0.0% those two score was not theirs either.** `station-hold` and
`learned-hold` read 0.0% on the bench, which had looked like two weak controllers.
Then: **`hold-station` was in neither suite.** Both controllers anybody has
deployed are station holds, and the bench contained nothing either of them could
do — a reach, a transect and a dock, asked of a controller whose whole job is to
stay put. Those zeros were guaranteed by the suite's composition rather than
earned, and a bench whose tasks exclude what its users have written is measuring
its own taste. It is first in both suites now.

That tripped a test worth tripping — "a benchmark whose contents move is not one" —
so the change is dated in the test rather than made quietly, along with what it
costs and what it does not. A **row** is named per task, so every row in the record
is still valid and every head-to-head comparison still holds; what is not
comparable across the change is a **suite aggregate**, which is over eight tasks
before and nine after.

**And then it was flown, which settles it:**

| controller | score | drift |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `station-hold` — deployed through the SDK | **100.0%** | 0.00 m |
| `pursue` — the built-in floor | **100.0%** | 0.00 m |
| `learned-hold` — deployed through the SDK | 12.3% | 0.01 m |

`station-hold` read **0.0%** on this bench yesterday and scores **100.0%** today,
level with the baseline, on the first occasion anybody asked it to do the thing it
was written for. Nothing about the controller changed. **A customer's controller was
being shown as worthless by a bench that never asked it to hold station** — which is
the worst thing a bench can do to the people it is for, and it took flying the
deployed controllers on their own task to see it.

`learned-hold`'s 12.3% is a real result and a more interesting one, and chasing it
produced the first genuine finding this bench has made about a controller rather
than about itself.

The obvious guess was the tether — trained in a tank that fitted none, flown on a
platform that has one. **Measured, and wrong:** the policy scores 1.000 with the
tether and 1.000 without it. What it actually is:

| controller | still | gentle (what the bench flies) | one knot (what it trained in) | two knots |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `learned-hold` | 0.139 | **0.210** | **1.000** | 0.058 |
| the hand-written hold | **1.000** | **1.000** | 0.094 | 0.040 |

**The trained policy learned a bias, not a controller.** It is perfect at exactly
the current it was trained in and poor in every other, including still water — a
linear policy told to fight one knot from the east applies that push whether or not
the knot is there, and in calm water it pushes itself off station. The bench flies
*gentle*, so it scored 0.210 in the tank and 12.3% on the platform, which agree.

The hand-written hold is the exact mirror: perfect in still and gentle, and it
fails from one knot up. So "learned 0.48 against hand-written 0.15" — the number
this repository has carried since 3 September as the demonstration that training
works — was true **only at the one current both were measured in**, and is the
least representative current of the four.

Two things follow, and neither is about the controller. Both are done.

**A single training condition produces a controller that wins exactly there and
loses everywhere else** — the oldest result in machine learning, and worth a line in
the SDK's own trainer rather than a rediscovery per customer. `examples/learn_hold.py`
now draws a current per episode from the four the bench knows, and scores a candidate
by its **worst** water rather than its mean: a mean would let a policy buy a perfect
knot with a hopeless calm, which is exactly the trade the first one made.

**And the bench flew one water**, so it could never have shown this. `--water` is on
both paths now, and the water is in the row's name — without that, two rows flown in
different water collide under one name, which is the same fault the hull and the reef
each had and the one that would have hidden this for good.

**Confirmed on the platform**, which is where it matters:

| row | score |
| --- | --- |
| `learned-hold` — the bench's own water, a gentle set | **12.3%** |
| `learned-hold in one-knot` — the water it was trained in | **100.0%** |
| `learned-hold in still` | **16.1%** |
| `pursue`, `station-hold` — gentle | 100.0% |

The tank predicted 1.000 / 0.210 / 0.139 for those three waters and the platform gave
100 / 12.3 / 16.1. Same shape, and the first water-dependent result this bench has
produced.

**And the first water matrix found a bug in the bench rather than a fact about a
controller.** `pursue` and `wary`, dry, came back at **100% in all four waters** —
still, gentle, one knot and two — which is too good, and it is. `bench dry` passed the
conditions **flat**: `{"currentMetresPerSecond": …}` where `read_conditions` looks in
`conditions["parameters"]`. So **every dry row ever flown was flown in still water
with no positioning fit**, whatever the bench said it had asked for, and `--water`
appeared to do nothing because it did nothing.

Which means the four-controller table at the top of this section was flown in still
water and not in a gentle set, and so was every other dry row before 27 September
2026. The platform path builds a conditions object through the API and was never
affected, so the platform rows stand. Fixed, with a test that holds the nesting
against the runtime's own reader so it cannot drift back.

**And it cost me a reversal I should not have made.** Two knots was taken out of the
trainer's water set as beyond the vehicle, which was right; I put it back on the
strength of that false 100%, which was wrong. A dive built by hand with the
conditions nested properly says `pursue` at two knots scores **0.02 and is swept 49
metres downstream**. The honest reading is the one I started with: still, gentle and
one knot are the waters a policy can be selected in, and two knots is a water to
report.

**Four times today an argument that felt complete lost to a measurement**, and this
one lost to a measurement that was itself wrong, which is the more useful lesson: a
number that is *too good* deserves the same suspicion as one that is too bad. Four
waters, four identical hundreds, and nothing in me twitched until the direct dive
disagreed.

**Re-measured, with the water actually applied**, and this is the bench's first real
water column — the runtime's own hold, which is what `pursue` delegates to for a
hold-station:

| water | the runtime's hold | the SDK's example, before | after |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| still | 100% | 1.000 | 1.000 |
| a gentle set | 100% | 1.000 | 1.000 |
| one knot | **95%** | 0.094 | **0.560** |
| two knots | **0%** | 0.040 | 0.038 |

Two knots is beyond this vehicle: the runtime's own hold is swept there too, 1.98 m of
drift and nothing on station. So it is a water to **report** and not one to select a
policy in, which is where this started.

And the SDK example is fixed. It was **1.60 m off station** at one knot and is now
**0.17 m**, measured against the truth — from a missing **integral** term and a windup
cap set too tight, both found by printing what the thing actually commanded: 11 N of a
145 N envelope, nowhere near saturated, with the carried error clamped flat. The cap is
now the axis's own authority rather than a number in metre-seconds that somebody has to
guess, which is the runtime hold's rule and removes a parameter nobody could set.

**And the gap to the runtime's hold was my arithmetic, not the controller.** 0.56
against 0.95 looked like a target; the 0.56 was a **30-second** tank run and the 0.95 a
**300-second** bench one, and this hold takes about thirteen seconds to get on station,
so the short run is mostly approach. Over the bench's own duration it scores **0.948
against 0.950**. Raising the integral gain to close a gap that was not there makes it
worse — 0.560, 0.530, 0.344, 0.241 as `positionKi` goes 0.30, 0.6, 1.0, 1.5 — which is
what the sweep was for.

**Fifth time today**, and the most quietly dangerous kind: two numbers of the same name
measured over different durations. Neither was wrong; putting them beside each other
was.

**And then the trained policy, retrained across the three waters it can hold in:**

| water | learned, retrained | learned, before | the hand-written hold |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| still | **1.000** | 0.139 | 1.000 |
| a gentle set | **1.000** | 0.210 | 1.000 |
| one knot | **1.000** | 1.000 | 0.560 |
| two knots (beyond the vehicle) | 0.058 | 0.058 | 0.038 |

That is the whole path this repository has claimed since 3 September, finally true:
a policy trained against the runtime's own physics on a laptop, written out as a
controller, deployed by digest and flown on the platform. It holds in **every water
it can**, and it beats the hand-written hold in the one where the hand-written hold
struggles — which is what "learned 0.48 against 0.15" was supposed to mean and did
not.

Three things had to be right first, and none of them was a week ago: the tank had to
fit the vehicle it claims to be (it fitted no tether), the trainer had to see more
than one water, and the water it cannot hold in had to be left out of the criterion
rather than allowed to flatten it.

**Deployed by digest as `stack_01M3HNBF4H86DGA6TM6B95H7YG` and flown on the
platform**, which is the only measurement that settles it:

| row | before | after |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `learned-hold` — the bench's own water | **12.3%** | **100.0%** |
| `learned-hold in still` | **16.1%** | **100.0%** |
| `learned-hold in one-knot` | 100.0% | 100.0% |

The tank said 1.000 / 1.000 / 1.000 and the platform said 100 / 100 / 100. A tank
that fits the vehicle predicts the platform, which is the whole reason to have
one — and the same tank a week ago said 1.000 for a policy that scored 12.3%
deployed.

The tank fits the package now, and takes `things=[…]` so a controller whose job is
not hitting things has something not to hit. With both, `avoid` was measured in the

| | struck | closest approach | ticks avoiding |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| sonar not fitted | **1073** | 1.5 m — straight through them | 0 |
| sonar fitted | **0** | **6.2 m — round them** | 1099 |

Same class, same frames, same path. The difference is only whether anything handed
it the fan.

**What is left.** Not the machinery — a deployed controller now gets the sonar, the
bottom and the objective, the tank fits the vehicle it claims to be, the bench has a
task the deployed controllers can do, and an arrangement can be put in front of any
of them from either bench. What is left is the part no amount of tooling supplies:
**a controller somebody outside this repository wrote**, flown against these
baselines. That is not a thing to build here.

The nearest thing to it that can be done here is `sonar-follow` — deployed since
30 August, the SDK-side counterpart of `wary`, and never flown. Now that a deployed
controller can actually see the fan, it is worth finding out whether it ever

read its altitude off the task: its first row was `struck 0` and nineteen metres
away, which measured nothing.

### 5 · Rehearse the dive before anyone gets on the boat

The operational sale rather than the research one. Fly it eight ways — half a
knot of current, the mooring thirty metres from where the chart says, the DVL
losing bottom lock over mud — and see which of those the day turns on.

**True today.** Missions carry doubts; the control plane crosses them into a
sweep and flies each; five missions exist across four places, including a
deep benthic transect whose first doubt is the one a pilot actually argues
about, two metres or four.

**Owed.**

  **The whole dive has been flown, and the sweep with it.** `missions/
  a-whole-dive-al-fahal.json` had existed unflown: descend to 3 m of altitude →
  an 80 m transect at 2 m → return and surface, crossed with three currents and
  whether the Doppler log keeps bottom track, twice each. Twelve dives of about
  2,520 simulated seconds.

  **It was unaffordable until it was undrawn.** Rendered, that sweep is roughly
  **49 hours**; the scenarios now say `pictures: false` and it is about two, on no
  graphics card, three dives at a time. Set in the control plane rather than asked
  of whoever writes the mission, because it is a property of sweeping and not of the
  mission — a scenario that explicitly asks for pictures keeps them, since a doubt
  is allowed to be about whether anybody could see what happened. Verified on the
  wire: the scenarios' containers run `/isaac-sim/python.sh` and their briefs say
  `drawn: false`.

  And `tools/rehearsal` has now been run on something other than a fixture. It
  reads the findings endpoint as it stands, and it says so when the answer is
  partial — *"11 scenarios are still in the water; this is the answer so far"* —
  which is the right behaviour for a page somebody might open while the ship is
  still being loaded.

  **What the sweep found.** Five of six scenarios survive at the 70% the mission
  asks for:

  | scenario | score | worst run |
  | --- | --- | --- |
  | a quarter knot · **no bottom track** | **61.1%** | 47.4% |
  | a tenth · no bottom track | 82.8% | 75.6% |
  | a quarter knot · the log holds | 93.1% | 92.8% |
  | a tenth · the log holds | 93.6% | 92.3% |
  | still · the log holds | 99.8% | 99.6% |
  | still · **no bottom track** | **99.9%** | 99.9% |

  Losing bottom track on the way down costs **nothing** in still water and is the
  difference between 93% and 61% in a quarter of a knot. It is the interaction, not
  either doubt on its own — which is the shape of finding a sweep exists to produce
  and the reason for flying the cross rather than one thing at a time.

  **And the page would not let me call that the finding.** One scenario had one run
  pass and one fail, so the threshold sits inside the run-to-run spread, and it says
  so: *"Every doubt separates only scenarios that could have gone either way, so
  there is nothing to say about which of them matters. Named, not ranked."* Two runs
  that disagree is a coin flip. That is the tool being more careful than its author,
  which is what it was written to be; the answer is more repeats.

  **Eight runs of that one scenario settle it: 2 survive of 8**, mean 63.9%, worst
  48.1%. So it is not a coin that landed badly — **a quarter of a knot with no bottom
  track on the way down fails this dive three times in four.** That is the sentence a
  rehearsal exists to produce, and it is worth a day of ship time: wait for bottom
  lock before committing to the descent, or expect to abort one run in four. Said
  before anybody is on the boat.

  Operationally, from twelve real whole dives: **16.2 Wh over 0.4 h** each, 14.8 of
  them on one charge, **1.1 ship days per working day** at 92% surviving — held back
  by the battery, and one scenario held back by the hull.

  **A dive is not yet a whole dive** — `r3`'s C1. The descent is now a task
  of its own, which it should always have been: a vehicle enters the water
  above the site and has to arrive at working altitude *over the site*, and
  between those two moments the current is setting it sideways and it cannot
  see the bottom to know. It is scored on arriving, on where it landed, and
  on arriving under control — a vehicle that arrives by falling stirs the
  bottom it came to photograph.

  And it reports, rather than scores, the fact every later leg inherits:
  **how much of the descent was flown blind**. A Doppler log has a range and
  above it there is no bottom track at all, so the vehicle is dead reckoning
  on a flight model through the part of the dive where the current is
  strongest. The metres it descended before lock, and the metres it moved
  sideways in them, are the honest size of the error the rest of the dive
  starts with — and no log from a real vehicle can say, because the vehicle
  does not know where it actually was. Bottom lock is handed down from the
  navigation rather than inferred from an altitude, so there is one answer
  to that question and not two.

  **Recovery** now reports itself: the depth it rose from, how fast it came
  up, how far off home it was when it surfaced, and how far the water
  carried it on the way — the ascent's version of the number the descent
  already gives, and one no real vehicle's log can produce. Reported and
  not scored: getting home and surfacing is the job, and every return in
  the record has to keep meaning what it meant.

  **Transit is `reach` and does not need a task of its own.** The **run-in**
  turned out not to need one either, once a stage stopped being measured
  from where the *dive* began — a transect after a descent now runs from
  where it arrives, along the heading it arrives on, which is what a run-in
  was for. Writing the first whole dive is what found that; the bug had been
  there the whole time and nothing noticed, because until there was a
  mission with more than one stage the two places were always the same.

**And the thing all four buyers actually have, which is not a dive.** KCRI
is putting two million corals into a hundred hectares by 2030. Red Sea
Global monitors three hundred sites on a repeat cycle. Beacon has relocated
two hundred and twenty-five thousand colonies by diver. NCW has four hundred
sites to visit a second time in a way that can be compared with the first.
None of them has the problem of one dive: they have **a grid, a fleet and a
boat calendar**, and no way to turn one into the others.

`tools/campaign` does that, and does it off evidence. The grid comes out of
the place's own seabed and its own colonies — cells that are mostly wet,
shaded by how many colonies are in them — so a cell described as twelve
metres deep with four hundred colonies is describing the reef that would
actually be flown. What a cell *costs* comes from a dive that was flown, or
from a sweep, and **the tool refuses to turn cells into boat days without
one**: cells times a number nobody measured is a spreadsheet, and a
spreadsheet about ship time is a promise. Looe Key, at forty metres a cell:
94 cells, 76,034 colonies, 58 vehicle hours, three working days on two
vehicles, five ship days once the weather takes its share.

The cells are worked in lanes rather than densest-first, because a boat that
hops to the best cell and then the next spends the day moving.

**Two constants of mine, replaced by the place's own figures.** Both were
found by pointing the tool at a real reef rather than a fixture.

*Which cells are the reef.* "Every cell with a colony in it" gave Al Fahal
**3,418 of 3,600 cells** — because four hundred thousand colonies scattered
over nine square kilometres put one in almost every cell, while the place's
own record says its reef is 269,804 m², three per cent of the site. A
programme planned off that would work thirty times the ground there is.
Now the place decides: the densest cells whose area comes to `reefAreaM2`.
108 cells.

*What counts as full.* A quarter of a colony a square metre sounded
cautious and would have called **every cell at Looe Key full**, which runs
at about half. Now it is a multiple of how thick the reef already is where
it grows, and the page says whether that came from the place or was chosen
because the place could not say.

The pattern is worth naming: every constant I picked was wrong for the
first real reef it met, and in both cases the place already knew the
answer.

**And for planting rather than surveying, a different order.** KCRI's gap
in their own words is *which cell to plant next given depth, substrate and
current*, and a place can answer two of those three. Depth comes straight
off its seabed, in a band, because outplanting is done in one. Substrate is
not inferred — it is **demonstrated**: a cell with four hundred colonies
already in it has hard bottom at a survivable depth, proven by the coral
that is on it, which is better evidence than a hardness field computed off
the shape of the ground and is the answer a reef ecologist gives when you
ask where to plant. The current it cannot answer, nothing in a place
records it, and the page says so rather than inventing a number for it.

Plus the caveat a spreadsheet would miss: a cell that is already full has
nowhere to put anything, so past a stated density the fuller cells rank
below emptier ones at the same depth.

**Both halves, on real data.** `pursue` flew the `survey` task at Looe Key
and covered **99.5%** of its rectangle — 7,165 of 7,200 cells, 1,500 s in
the water, navigation 0.92 m out on average. Everything downstream then
came from that dive and nothing from a number anybody picked:

| | | |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dives a cell takes | from a flown dive | 0.89 — a 40 m cell against the 1,791 m² the survey's camera actually covered |
| Dives, all of it | derived | 84 |
| A cell takes | from a flown dive | 0.37 h — what the dive spent in the water, end to end |
| Vehicle hours | derived | 35 |
| Working days | derived | 4 |

And the proof underneath: **2 of 94 cells covered, 92 missed, 0 covered
that were not in the plan.** One survey is two cells of a ninety-four-cell
programme, and the page says so in red rather than letting a 99.5% score
read as a job done.

**And afterwards, the half that makes it a product.** `--flown` reads back
what the missions themselves produced — the ground a survey's camera
actually passed over, the corals that actually went in — and bins it onto
the same cells the plan was made on. Same grid, before and after, which is
the only way the two are comparable. It reports cells covered, cells
**missed**, and ground passed over that was never in the plan: a transit
crosses cells on its way, and calling that coverage would inflate the one
number the page exists to be truthful about.

A missed cell is drawn red, not left blank. Every one of these programmes
can tell you what they set out to do; none of them can hand a regulator a
map of what they actually covered, and a page that let a gap look like
water would be worse than not having one.

**And round two, held against round one.** NCW covered four hundred sites
in 2024–25 and what they need next is a second round that can be compared
with the first; Red Sea Global has the same problem three hundred times a
cycle. `tools/again` asks two questions and refuses to merge them. *The
same ground*: which cells each round reached, on one grid, so a cell
covered then and missed now shows as a hole in the series rather than as
nothing. *Seen the same way*: the task, the altitude and the swath, because
**a round flown at four metres where the last was at two is not a repeat,
and the difference will read as change on the reef** — which is the most
expensive mistake a monitoring programme can make, since it is invisible
and it points at a finding.

A round that did several things is not averaged into one: an average
altitude is a number no dive was flown at, and it says "cannot say" rather
than inventing one.

**It has now been run on two real rounds, and this is what it said.** Two Looe
Key surveys, the same plan, different seeds, flown a day apart:

    looe-key: round two against round one
      8 cells in both · 0 covered then and missed now · 0 new
      what was flown                 survey → survey   the same
      how high it was flown             5.0 → 5.0      the same
      how wide the camera saw           8.6 → 8.61     the same

"Same ground, seen the same way" — which is the answer NCW needs before anybody
argues about whether the coral changed. And the page states the thing a summary
would have hidden: **8 cells of 323, two percent of the grid.** One
twenty-five-minute survey covers two percent of this site at 20 m cells, so a
round that covers Looe Key is about fifty dives — which is item 3's arithmetic
arriving from the other direction.

Two faults came out of running it, both invisible to fixtures. It named the reef
after the *directory* it was pointed at, so a package read as
`ver_01M2NCGTERKN5Q1GQE61R6VGP9` and two builds of one reef would have headed two
pages as two reefs — the exact confusion the tool exists to catch in somebody
else's data. And it **computed the whole comparison and then died writing it
out**: `covered_by` hands back its coverage keyed by the cell itself, a `(row,
column)` tuple, which is the right shape for drawing a grid and not a shape JSON
has — a tuple cannot be an object key, and `json.dumps`'s `default=` never sees it
because that handles values and not keys. Every earlier run had zero cells in
common, so nothing had ever reached the line. And it does not score the reef — whether the coral
changed is a question for the data the rounds produced, and this says
whether that question can honestly be asked of them.

  Weather *history* has started: `asItWasOn(record, date)` composes observed
  conditions from a past day of the record — that day's temperature with that
  day's instant on it. What it will not do is fill in the rest. The daily
  series carries no wave and no wind, because a buoy's are readings and not a
  history, so a dive flown from it reads: temperature **measured**, sea state
  **nobody said**, current **nobody said**. That is the truth about what
  anybody knows about last Tuesday, and the page from item 2 now shows it as
  such. A day nobody recorded is refused rather than served from the day
  before wearing Tuesday's date.
  **The answer, as a page** — this was already written down as owed and it is
  not. `What(flown, good)` ranks the doubts by how much each *changes* the
  outcome rather than by what was present when things failed, names the ones
  that made no difference without ranking them, says which sentence the plan
  turns on, and refuses to rank a doubt whose scenarios could have gone
  either way. The Sweeps screen shows all of it: the answer, what it costs,
  every scenario. It even warns when the runs were not all computed by the
  same physics.

  The *sendable* version is now `tools/rehearsal`: the same answer as one
  page, in the same house style as the place pages and the mission report —
  what it survives, the sentence the day turns on, the doubts ranked by how
  much each changes the outcome, what made no difference named without being
  ranked, what it costs, and every scenario underneath. A screen is for the
  person who ran the sweep; the decision is made by somebody who will not
  open the application.

  It keeps the analysis's two refusals, because they are the reason to trust
  it: a doubt whose scenarios could all have gone either way is **named and
  not ranked**, and runs that were not all computed by the same physics are
  **not one table** — the page says so at the top rather than averaging
  across them.

  Pointed at a real sweep in the record — *Outplant B7, three times each*,
  twelve runs — it comes back with an operational answer rather than a
  grid: survives **1 of 4** scenarios, **turns on the current** (both
  scenarios with it fail and nothing in the list rescues them), a dive is
  5.8 Wh over twelve minutes, thirty-nine of them fit in an eight-hour day,
  and at 33% of attempts surviving that is **three ship days for one
  working day**. It also refuses to rank the second doubt, because every
  scenario that one separates could have gone either way. That is the page
  for KCRI, and it came out of a sweep flown a fortnight ago.

  One stylesheet now, not three. The place page, the index and the mission
  report each carried their own near-identical copy, and a house style that
  has to be copied stops being one. These pages go in front of the same
  people, often on the same day.

### 6 · The physics has been held against something

Not a selling point on its own. It is what stops the other five being
dismissed.

**True today.** `mhl-tank` is the only geometry here that is known rather than
derived, and `tools/tow` puts two numbers on the hull: the simulator obeys its
own table to three parts in a thousand, and the table itself sits inside what
a bluff open frame of the vehicle's published dimensions gives. Deliberately
not checked against a flat-plate friction line, which would be an order of
magnitude low and a false pass.

**Owed.** A real carriage run on a real BlueROV2 to replace the estimate. That
is somebody else's tank and somebody else's afternoon, and it is worth asking
KAUST for — **it is also a reason for them to talk to us.**

### 7 · Any vehicle, the same mission

**True today.** A BlueROV2 and a Seaglider fly the same missions and are
scored the same way; the glider has no thrusters at all and the platform does
not assume it does. Vehicle packages carry mass, inertia, added mass, damping,
thrusters, sensors, power, lights and a topic contract.

**A hull that is not ours can now be flown by an SDK controller.** It could
not be, and the failure was the same shape as the two interfaces: the SDK's
list of vehicles is generated from *this repository's* catalogue at build
time, so a customer could publish their own hull to the platform — which is
what a catalogue is for — and then be told by the SDK that there was no such
vehicle. A package can now carry its own `vehicle.json`, and
`CORAL_CITY_VEHICLES` points at where those packages are.

The card is written by `generate_vehicles.py --package`, which asks the
**runtime's own allocator** what the hull can produce on each axis. The SDK
reads that number and refuses a card without it rather than working it out:
a capability the SDK computed for itself would be a second implementation
of the arithmetic the runtime uses to fly the thing, and two implementations
of one idea agree until they do not.

Still owed there: the platform serving that card with the vehicle, so a
controller author does not have to have the package on disk.

**The multibeam is the instrument these programmes actually survey with**,
and it is now fitted, modelled and productive. A separate class from the
forward-looking sonar on purpose: they answer different questions and fail
differently — one wants the nearest return in each direction and treats the
bottom as an obstacle, the other wants *the bottom* in each direction with
the sounding's position attached, and a return off a coral head is a
sounding rather than something to steer around.

What is modelled is the geometry and the errors that decide whether a
survey is any good: where each beam lands, how wide the swath is from an
altitude (the number a line plan is made of), and **how a sounding gets
worse off nadir** — 1/cos, the extra path through water whose sound speed
nobody knows exactly. A fixed error would say a beam at sixty degrees is as
good as one straight down, which is the single thing everybody who has used
one knows to be false. What is not modelled is the acoustics; those decide
whether a real instrument gets a return at all, and what is here decides
whether a survey *plan* is any good, which is a geometry question.

It is fitted to the **REMUS**, which is what carries one. It went on the
BlueROV2 first, and that was wrong twice over: a stock BlueROV2 has no
multibeam, and declaring one quietly added twenty-eight watts to the hotel
load of every dive the platform has ever flown that hull on. A survey head
goes on a survey AUV.

A survey now leaves `bathymetry.asc` — the soundings gridded, in the format
every GIS reads. A cell with no sounding is NODATA and not an
interpolation: a chart that looks complete and is not is worse than one
with holes, because somebody navigates off it.

**Side-scan is the other half and it needs something first.** It images the
*texture* of the bottom rather than its depth, and doing that honestly
means backscatter against grazing angle and substrate — which the platform
already knows, because `zonation` computes a hardness field for every place
and `make-site` writes it as a raster. It just does not ship it: the place
carries its heightfield and its coral and not its hardness. So side-scan
waits on a place that carries its substrate, which is a rebuild of every
place and not a thing to start while they are being restored.

**The Heavy and the REMUS are published.** They had been written and
unpublished since they were added, and finding out why took the same shape
as everything else today: `tools/publish vehicle ... --publish`, the
command written at the top of that file, uploaded the package and was then
refused — "a vehicle version states how it moves before it can be
published" — because nothing ever sent the dynamics, and the message named
no file. It sends them now, out of the package's own `dynamics.json`,
filtered to the summary the platform keeps; a test holds that list against
the Go struct's own tags, because a list that drifted would refuse every
new vehicle with a message about a field nobody had heard of.

**And somebody else can fly them.** Publishing your own vehicle is only
half of it; the other half is the SDK being able to describe a hull it has
never seen. It can, and it needed no new endpoint: the card is a file in
the package, `tools/publish` uploads every file in the directory, and the
platform already serves a version's files. Three places, in order — the
hulls generated into the SDK, a package on disk, then the platform. Proved
against the box with nothing on disk: `remus-100` comes back as 30.48 kg
with one propeller that accepts thruster commands, `bluerov2-heavy` as
eight thrusters and 146 N of surge.

**Flown, and it took no GPU time at all.** That was the estimate when this was
written; a dry bench needs no card. The Heavy and the REMUS both flew the quick
suite over Al Fahal's own heightfield, and the REMUS — a torpedo hull that cannot
hover — completed all three tasks, drifting **2.84 m against the BlueROV2's 4.01**,
which is the better navigation of a survey AUV showing up where it should.

It also found something the rows themselves could not say: **neither the Heavy nor
the REMUS had a battery.** Both packages state a hotel load and no capacity, so every
bench row for either read `0.00 Wh` — not "unknown" but "this controller used no
energy", in the column somebody comparing controllers looks at first. The Heavy now
carries the standard BlueROV2 pack, because it is the same battery in the same
enclosure and the Heavy Retrofit changes the thrusters and the buoyancy; that is
recorded as derived. The REMUS still states none, because this repository has no
source for its published energy, and the column reads `—`.

**And re-flown across the water axis**, now that `--water` works and the dry bench
applies it. Holding station, one controller, three hulls, four waters:

| hull | still | gentle | one knot | two knots |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| BlueROV2 | 100% | 100% | **95%** | 0% |
| BlueROV2 Heavy | 100% | 100% | **72%** | 0% |
| REMUS 100 | **2%** | **2%** | **2%** | 0% |

Three things in that table, and none of them was knowable before today.

**The REMUS cannot hold station at all** — 2% in *still water*, and the same 1.04 m of
drift in every column. That is not a broken hull, it is the right answer: a torpedo AUV
has no lateral or vertical thrusters and flies to stay up. It is the plainest possible
demonstration of what "any vehicle, the same mission" is for — the mission is wrong for
the hull, and the table says so instead of the vehicle looking bad.

**The Heavy is worse at one knot than the standard hull**, 72% against 95%, which is
the opposite of what eight thrusters suggests and worth knowing before anybody
specifies one for current-swept work.

**And the Heavy spends 149.5 Wh failing to hold at two knots** against the BlueROV2's
10.2 — more than half of a 266 Wh pack, in five minutes, to end up nowhere. That number
only exists because the Heavy was given the battery it had been missing (above); before
that the column read `0.00 Wh`. The REMUS column reads `—`, which is what an unknown
looks like when it is not pretending to be a zero.

**Owed.** Side-scan, which waits on a place carrying its substrate (above).

## The order

**In the order they are numbered, one to seven.** The case for starting at
item 4 is that it is the least true thing we say; the case for starting at

## What the 26 September delete left, exactly

Worth writing down, because the plan above describes places that are not
quite the places on the box any more.

**Flyable: all six.** Their published packages were in object storage,
which survived, and the record never lost them. A dive can be defined and

**On disk: two, and now six.** Only `looe-key` and `shushah` had a source

cannot be made again; it is a day of machine time, not a day of thinking.

## The rule, carried forward unchanged

A tool that cannot do its job must fail, not report. When a change produces no
visible difference, the first hypothesis is that the change did not arrive.
And a number with no provenance is a number that will be cited back as though
this platform had measured it.

To which this plan adds one, learned four times in a week:

**Two implementations of one idea will agree until they do not, and the
disagreement will look like a finding.** Two cover functions, two hardness
functions, two `Command` classes. The first two cost days. The third has not
cost anything yet.
